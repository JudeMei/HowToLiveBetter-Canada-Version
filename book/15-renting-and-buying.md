# 15. Renting & Buying a Home

> Housing is the largest expense for most Canadians. This chapter covers your rights as a tenant and how to protect your money when buying.

---

### 1. Know if your apartment is rent-controlled
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: In Ontario, apartments occupied for the first time *after* Nov 15, 2018, have NO rent control. The landlord can raise the rent by $1,000/month if they want to at the end of the lease. Older buildings are capped at a provincial guideline (usually 1-2.5% per year). Know exactly what you are moving into.
- Payoff: Avoids being financially forced out of your home after one year.
- Evidence: A (Provincial Law)
- Source: Residential Tenancies Act (Ontario).
- Note: Rent control laws vary wildly by province. BC has rent control tied to the unit. Alberta has no limit on the amount a landlord can increase rent (though they can only do it once a year). Check your provincial rules before signing a lease.

### 2. Never pay a security deposit in cash without a receipt
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Rental scams are rampant. Fake landlords post properties they don't own, take your deposit, and disappear. Demand a receipt, verify the owner (if possible), and view the apartment in person before handing over money.
- Payoff: Prevents losing first/last month's rent (often $3,000+).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre.
- Note: In Ontario, landlords cannot legally ask for "damage deposits," only a rent deposit (last month's rent) and a key deposit (which must be refundable). In BC and Alberta, damage deposits are legal but capped (usually at half a month's rent).

### 3. A lease does not automatically end when the one-year term is up
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: In most provinces (like Ontario and BC), when a one-year lease ends, it automatically converts to a month-to-month tenancy. You do NOT have to sign a new lease, and the landlord cannot kick you out just because the lease ended.
- Payoff: Prevents illegal evictions and forced rent hikes.
- Evidence: A (Provincial Law)
- Source: Provincial tenancy boards.
- Note: A landlord can only evict you for specific, legally allowed reasons (e.g., non-payment of rent, or the landlord/purchaser moving in). "The lease is up" is not a legal reason to evict in most provinces.

### 4. Get a home inspection before buying (or include it as a condition)
- Cost: $400–$600.
- Plain language: A house might look beautiful but have a cracked foundation, mold in the attic, or Knob and Tube wiring (which is uninsurable). An inspection tells you what you are actually buying.
- Payoff: Prevents buying a "money pit" that requires $50,000 in immediate hidden repairs.
- Evidence: B
- Note: In extremely hot seller's markets, buyers sometimes waive this condition to win bidding wars. This is an massive financial risk. If you must waive it, bring an inspector or knowledgeable contractor with you to the viewing.

### 5. Buy Title Insurance when purchasing a home
- Cost: ~$250–$500 (one-time premium, usually arranged by your lawyer at closing).
- Plain language: Title insurance protects you if someone commits title fraud (steals your house by forging documents and taking out a mortgage on it), or if a previous owner did unpermitted work that the city is now forcing you to tear down.
- Payoff: Protects the single largest asset you will ever own from catastrophic fraud or legal claims.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA).
- Note: This is different from mortgage default insurance (CMHC), which protects the *bank* if you don't pay. Title insurance protects *you*.

### 6. Use the First Home Savings Account (FHSA) and Home Buyers' Plan (HBP)
- Cost: Free to use.
- Plain language: The FHSA allows you to save $40,000 tax-free for a down payment (deductible contributions, tax-free withdrawals). The HBP allows you to borrow up to $60,000 (as of 2024) from your RRSP tax-free to buy your first home, provided you pay it back over 15 years.
- Payoff: Tens of thousands in tax savings, accelerating your ability to save a down payment.
- Evidence: A (Tax Law)
- Source: CRA.
- Note: You can use both the FHSA and the HBP for the same home purchase.
