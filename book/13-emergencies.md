# 13. Emergencies: What to Do First

> When seconds count, you won't have time to Google what to do. Memorize these protocols.

---

### 1. Call 911 first (unless performing CPR alone on a child/infant)
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: In almost every emergency, activating the emergency response system is your first priority.
- Payoff: Dispatches professional help and gets you a 911 operator who will talk you through first aid/CPR until paramedics arrive.
- Evidence: A
- Note: The only major exception: if you are alone with an infant/child who has suffered a cardiac arrest (usually due to choking or drowning), do 2 minutes of CPR *before* leaving them to call 911. For adults, call 911 immediately.

### 2. Cardiac Arrest: Push hard and fast in the center of the chest
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: If someone collapses and is unresponsive and not breathing normally (or just gasping), assume cardiac arrest. Put the heel of your hand on the center of their chest, put your other hand on top, and push down 2 inches at a rate of 100-120 beats per minute (the beat of "Stayin' Alive").
- Payoff: Doubles or triples the chance of survival.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada.
- Note: Do not stop compressions unless an AED is analyzing or the person wakes up. You do not need to do rescue breaths if you aren't trained.

### 3. Opioid Overdose: Administer Naloxone
- Cost: Free (kits available at pharmacies).
- Plain language: Unresponsive, slow/shallow breathing, blue/grey lips or fingertips, pinpoint pupils. Call 911, administer Naloxone (nasal spray or injection), and begin CPR if they are not breathing.
- Payoff: Reverses the overdose and prevents death from respiratory failure.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Health Canada.
- Note: The Good Samaritan Drug Overdose Act protects anyone who calls 911 for an overdose from charges for simple possession of illegal drugs. Don't let fear of arrest stop you from calling 911.

### 4. Stroke: Think FAST
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: **F**ace drooping, **A**rm weakness, **S**peech difficulty, **T**ime to call 911.
- Payoff: Getting the person to the hospital within 3.5 hours allows for clot-busting drugs that dramatically reduce long-term disability.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada.
- Note: Do NOT let the person drive themselves to the hospital. Do NOT give them Aspirin (if the stroke is hemorrhagic, Aspirin will make the bleeding worse).

### 5. Heart Attack: Chew Aspirin
- Cost: $5 for a bottle of Aspirin.
- Plain language: Chest pain, shortness of breath, pain radiating to the arm/jaw, nausea. Call 911. If the dispatcher advises it, have the person chew and swallow one regular Aspirin (325mg) or two low-dose Aspirins (81mg each).
- Payoff: Aspirin inhibits platelets and prevents the blood clot in the heart from getting larger. Studies show early Aspirin reduces heart attack mortality by 23%.
- Evidence: A
- Source: ISIS-2 Collaborative Group.
- Note: Must be Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), not Tylenol (acetaminophen) or Advil (ibuprofen). Must chew it to absorb it faster. Do not give if they are allergic or bleeding heavily.

### 6. Severe Bleeding: Apply direct pressure and use a tourniquet for limbs
- Cost: $30 for a CAT tourniquet (keep in your car/first aid kit).
- Plain language: If blood is spurting or pooling rapidly from an arm or leg, apply direct, hard pressure with a cloth. If it doesn't stop, apply a tourniquet 2-3 inches above the wound, tighten until the bleeding stops, and note the time.
- Payoff: Prevents death from exsanguination (bleeding out).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Stop the Bleed campaign (American College of Surgeons).
- Note: Improvised tourniquets (belts, shirts) rarely work well. Buy a real CAT (Combat Application Tourniquet) or SOF-T tourniquet from a reputable dealer (beware of Amazon fakes).

### 7. Fire: Get out, stay low, close doors behind you
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Smoke is toxic and kills faster than flames. Stay low to the ground where the air is cleaner. Close doors behind you to slow the spread of the fire.
- Payoff: Increases survival time. A closed solid-core door can hold back a fire for 15-20 minutes.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Fire Services across Canada.
- Note: Once you are out, stay out. Never go back in for pets or property.

### 8. Anaphylaxis: Use the EpiPen, then call 911
- Cost: $100-$150 for an epinephrine auto-injector.
- Plain language: Severe allergic reaction (swelling of throat, hives, difficulty breathing). Inject the EpiPen into the outer thigh (through clothing if necessary), hold for 3 seconds. Call 911 immediately.
- Payoff: Epinephrine is the only drug that halts anaphylaxis. Antihistamines (like Benadryl) will not save their life.
- Evidence: A
- Note: The effects of the EpiPen may wear off after 10-15 minutes, requiring a second dose or immediate hospital treatment. Always go to the hospital.
