# 6. Things That Don't Work

> This chapter covers common products, treatments, and ideas that are widely promoted but lack evidence of effectiveness, or are actively harmful. Save your money and energy.

---

### 1. Don't waste money on multivitamin supplements (unless deficient or pregnant)
- Cost: Saves $100–$300/year.
- Plain language: For healthy adults, multivitamins do not prevent cancer, heart disease, or early death. You are just making expensive urine.
- Payoff: A massive meta-analysis (Fortmann SP et al., 2013, Annals of Internal Medicine, reviewing 27 trials) found no evidence that vitamin and mineral supplements reduce mortality, cardiovascular disease, or cancer in healthy populations without nutritional deficiencies.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Fortmann SP et al. (2013). Vitamin and mineral supplements in the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease and cancer: An updated systematic evidence review for the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force. Ann Intern Med. 159(12):824-834.
- Note: Exceptions: Folic acid is critical for women planning pregnancy or who are pregnant (prevents neural tube defects). Vitamin D is recommended for Canadians during winter (1000-2000 IU/day) because we don't get enough sun. Vitamin B12 is necessary for vegans. Iron is necessary if a blood test shows deficiency.

### 2. Don't use chiropractic for anything other than specific types of lower back pain
- Cost: Saves money; avoids risk of catastrophic injury.
- Plain language: There is no evidence chiropractic adjustments can treat asthma, ear infections, ADHD, or general health issues. Neck adjustments carry a rare but severe risk of causing a stroke (arterial dissection).
- Payoff: A Cochrane review (Rubinstein SM et al., 2012) found that spinal manipulative therapy is no more effective than conventional medical care (exercise, pain relievers) for acute low back pain. For non-musculoskeletal conditions, a systematic review (Ernst E, 2009) found zero evidence of benefit. The risk of cervical artery dissection following neck manipulation is documented in neurological literature.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Rubinstein SM et al. (2012). Spinal manipulative therapy for acute low-back pain. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008880.pub2; Ernst E. (2009). Chiropractic treatment for gastrointestinal problems: a systematic review of clinical trials. Can J Gastroenterol.
- Note: If you have lower back pain, physiotherapy (focused on active movement and strengthening) has better long-term evidence than passive adjustments. Never let anyone rapidly twist your neck.

### 3. Don't buy "detox" teas, juices, or supplements
- Cost: Saves money.
- Plain language: "Detoxing" is a marketing myth. You already have a highly efficient detox system: your liver and your kidneys. If they stop working, you need a hospital, not a juice cleanse.
- Payoff: Saves money. Many detox products are essentially expensive laxatives (e.g., senna tea) that cause dehydration and electrolyte imbalance.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Klein AV, Kiat H. (2015). Detox diets for toxin elimination and weight management: a critical review of the evidence. J Hum Nutr Diet. 28(6):675-86.
- Note: The best way to "detox" is to drink water, eat fiber, stop drinking alcohol, and let your liver do its job.

### 4. Don't rely on homeopathy
- Cost: Saves money; prevents dangerous delays in real medical treatment.
- Plain language: Homeopathy is chemically identical to plain water or sugar pills. Its central premise ("like cures like" diluted to the point where zero molecules of the original substance remain) violates the laws of physics and chemistry.
- Payoff: The Australian National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC, 2015) conducted the most comprehensive review of homeopathy to date (1,800 papers) and concluded there are no health conditions for which there is reliable evidence that homeopathy is effective.
- Evidence: A
- Source: NHMRC. (2015). NHMRC Information Paper: Evidence on the effectiveness of homeopathy for treating health conditions.
- Note: Homeopathic products in Canada have a DIN-HM on the label. Health Canada approves them based on "homeopathic principles" (traditional use), NOT on scientific proof that they work. Do not confuse homeopathy with herbal medicine (which sometimes contains active pharmacological compounds).

### 5. Don't rely on naturopathy for serious illness (especially cancer)
- Cost: Saves thousands of dollars; prevents premature death.
- Plain language: While naturopaths can give decent advice on diet and lifestyle, their treatments for serious diseases like cancer (e.g., high-dose IV vitamin C, ozone therapy) lack evidence and can interfere with life-saving chemotherapy.
- Payoff: Studies show that cancer patients who choose alternative medicine as their primary treatment have significantly higher mortality rates. A study (Johnson SB et al., 2018, JAMA Oncology) found patients who used complementary medicine were more likely to refuse conventional treatment, leading to a 2-fold greater risk of death.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Johnson SB et al. (2018). Complementary Medicine, Refusal of Conventional Cancer Therapy, and Survival Among Patients With Curable Cancers. JAMA Oncol. 4(10):1375-1381. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaoncol.2018.2487
- Note: If you have cancer, follow an oncologist.

### 6. Don't buy alkaline water or follow the "alkaline diet" for health
- Cost: Saves money.
- Plain language: You cannot change the pH of your blood by eating or drinking. If you could, you would die. Your lungs and kidneys tightly regulate blood pH between 7.35 and 7.45.
- Payoff: A systematic review (Fenton TR, Huang T, 2016, BMJ Open) found no evidence that an alkaline diet or alkaline water prevents or treats cancer. The acid-ash hypothesis of osteoporosis has also been debunked.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Fenton TR, Huang T. (2016). Systematic review of the association between dietary acid load, alkaline water and cancer. BMJ Open. 6(6):e010438.
- Note: Alkaline water is just expensive water.

### 7. Essential oils are not medicine
- Cost: Saves money.
- Plain language: Essential oils smell nice and can be relaxing, but they do not cure viral infections, fix hormonal imbalances, or treat serious illnesses.
- Payoff: Prevents skin irritation and toxicity (if ingested). There is no high-quality clinical evidence supporting essential oils for disease treatment.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Various systematic reviews on aromatherapy show some benefit for subjective anxiety/relaxation, but no objective medical outcomes.
- Note: Never ingest essential oils, and keep them away from pets (many are highly toxic to cats and dogs).
