# 12. Starting a Business

> Most small businesses fail within five years. This chapter covers how to protect your personal assets when starting out, and the regulatory requirements you cannot ignore.

---

### 1. Incorporate to protect your personal assets
- Cost: ~$200 for federal incorporation (plus annual filings), or up to $1,500 if using a lawyer.
- Plain language: If you operate as a "Sole Proprietor," you and the business are the same legal entity. If the business is sued or goes bankrupt, they take your house and your car. A Corporation is a separate legal entity; if it goes bankrupt, your personal assets are generally protected.
- Payoff: Limits your personal liability to the amount you invested in the company.
- Evidence: A (Corporate Law)
- Source: Canada Business Corporations Act (CBCA); provincial equivalents.
- Note: Exceptions: Directors can be held personally liable for unpaid employee wages, unpaid HST/GST, and environmental damage. And if a bank asks you for a "personal guarantee" on a business loan, the corporate veil won't protect you from that debt.

### 2. You must register for GST/HST when you hit $30,000 in revenue
- Cost: Zero to register. Requires filing returns.
- Plain language: Once your business bills $30,000 in a single calendar quarter or over four consecutive quarters, you are no longer a "small supplier." You must register for a GST/HST number, charge tax to your Canadian clients, and remit it to the CRA.
- Payoff: Avoids massive tax penalties. If you fail to register and don't charge the tax, the CRA will audit you and demand the uncollected tax out of your own pocket, plus interest and penalties.
- Evidence: A (Excise Tax Act)
- Source: CRA. Registering for a GST/HST account.
- Note: You can register voluntarily before hitting $30,000. Doing so allows you to claim Input Tax Credits (ITCs) to recover the GST/HST you pay on business expenses.

### 3. Keep business and personal finances completely separate
- Cost: Free (open a separate business bank account).
- Plain language: Never pay for groceries with the business debit card, and never deposit a client cheque into your personal account.
- Payoff: Makes tax filing 10x easier, proves to the CRA that the business is legitimate, and preserves the "corporate veil" (liability protection) if you are incorporated.
- Evidence: A (Accounting standards)
- Note: Use accounting software (like QuickBooks or Xero) from day one.

### 4. Understand the difference between an Employee and an Independent Contractor
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: You cannot hire someone to work 40 hours a week, use your equipment, and take your orders, and just call them an "independent contractor" to avoid paying CPP, EI, and vacation pay. The CRA determines their status based on the reality of the working relationship, not the title you gave them.
- Payoff: Avoids devastating CRA audits where you are forced to pay back-taxes, CPP, and EI premiums with penalties.
- Evidence: A (Tax Law)
- Source: CRA. Employee or Self-employed? (RC4110).
- Note: If you control how, when, and where the work is done, they are an employee.

### 5. Get liability insurance
- Cost: $500–$2,000/year for most small businesses.
- Plain language: Commercial General Liability (CGL) covers you if someone slips in your store or your product injures someone. Professional Liability (Errors & Omissions) covers you if you give bad professional advice and a client sues for financial loss.
- Payoff: Prevents bankruptcy from a single lawsuit.
- Evidence: B
- Note: Do not assume your home insurance covers your home-based business. Usually, it specifically excludes business activities unless you buy a rider.
