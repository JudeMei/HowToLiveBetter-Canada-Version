# 25. After Someone Dies

> The logistics of death are overwhelming. This chapter covers the immediate steps and financial entitlements for survivors in Canada.

---

### 1. Get the Medical Certificate of Death and Death Certificate
- Cost: $15–$50 per copy of the Death Certificate (order multiple).
- Plain language: When someone dies, a doctor or coroner issues a Medical Certificate of Death. The funeral director usually uses this to register the death with the province. You then must order official Death Certificates from the province.
- Payoff: You cannot close bank accounts, cancel cell phones, or claim life insurance without an official Death Certificate.
- Evidence: A (Provincial Administrative Procedure)
- Source: Provincial Vital Statistics agencies.
- Note: Order at least 4-5 original copies. Many institutions will not accept a photocopy.

### 2. Apply for the CPP Death Benefit
- Cost: Free to apply.
- Plain language: If the deceased contributed to the Canada Pension Plan (CPP) for a minimum amount of time (usually 3 years or 1/3 of their working life), their estate is entitled to a one-time, flat-rate payment of $2,500.
- Payoff: Helps cover funeral expenses.
- Evidence: A (Federal Policy)
- Source: Service Canada. CPP Death Benefit.
- Note: It is not automatic. The executor (or next of kin) must apply within 60 days of the death. It is taxable income for the estate.

### 3. Apply for CPP Survivor's Pension and Children's Benefit
- Cost: Free to apply.
- Plain language: If the deceased contributed to CPP, their surviving spouse (or common-law partner) is entitled to a monthly pension. Dependent children (under 18, or under 25 and in school full-time) are also entitled to a monthly flat-rate benefit.
- Payoff: Long-term financial support for the surviving family.
- Evidence: A (Federal Policy)
- Source: Service Canada. CPP Survivor's Pension.
- Note: Apply immediately. Retroactive payments are limited to a maximum of 12 months.

### 4. Notify the CRA and Service Canada to stop benefit payments
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: You must notify the CRA and Service Canada (OAS, CPP, EI) immediately when someone dies. If you don't, they will keep depositing benefit cheques, and eventually, the government will demand all that money back with interest.
- Payoff: Prevents an administrative and financial nightmare for the estate.
- Evidence: A
- Source: CRA. What to do when someone has died.
- Note: Any OAS or CPP payments received for the month *after* the person died must be returned.

### 5. Do not pay the deceased's debts out of your own pocket
- Cost: Saves your personal wealth.
- Plain language: When someone dies, their debts belong to their "Estate." If the estate has no money, the debts die with the person. Unless you co-signed the loan, YOU are not responsible for their credit card debt, mortgage, or personal loans.
- Payoff: Protects you from aggressive creditors.
- Evidence: A (Estate Law)
- Note: Collection agencies may try to pressure grieving relatives into paying. Tell them the person is deceased, give them the executor's contact info, and hang up. Do not distribute the estate's money to heirs until all debts and taxes are paid, or the executor becomes personally liable.

### 6. Understand Probate
- Cost: Probate fees/taxes (e.g., ~1.5% in Ontario) plus legal fees.
- Plain language: Probate (or Certificate of Appointment of Estate Trustee) is the court process that validates the Will and gives the executor the legal authority to deal with the assets (like selling the house or accessing large bank accounts).
- Payoff: Legal authority to settle the estate.
- Evidence: A (Estate Law)
- Note: Assets with a named beneficiary (like an RRSP, TFSA, or life insurance policy) bypass probate and go directly to the beneficiary, saving time and probate taxes. Jointly owned assets (like a joint bank account or a house owned as "joint tenants") also bypass probate and go to the surviving owner.
