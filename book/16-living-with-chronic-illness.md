# 16. Living with a Chronic Illness

> Managing a chronic condition is a part-time job. This chapter covers how to navigate the Canadian healthcare system efficiently and access financial supports.

---

### 1. Apply for the Disability Tax Credit (DTC) if eligible
- Cost: Free to apply (your doctor may charge a small fee to fill out the form).
- Plain language: The DTC is a non-refundable tax credit for Canadians with a severe and prolonged impairment. It reduces the income tax you pay by about $1,500–$2,000/year. More importantly, it opens the door to the Registered Disability Savings Plan (RDSP).
- Payoff: Significant tax savings; access to massive government matching grants via the RDSP.
- Evidence: A (Tax Law)
- Source: CRA. Disability Tax Credit.
- Note: You do not need to be completely unable to work to qualify. The criteria focus on a marked restriction in basic activities of daily living (e.g., walking, feeding, dressing, mental functions) or requiring life-sustaining therapy (e.g., Type 1 diabetes). The CRA allows you to claim it retroactively for up to 10 years.

### 2. Keep your own medical records
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Provincial health systems do not talk to each other perfectly, and even within a province, hospitals and family doctors often use different electronic systems. Keep a binder or secure digital folder with your bloodwork, imaging reports, and specialist letters.
- Payoff: Saves months of waiting for records to be transferred; prevents duplicate testing; improves care when seeing a new specialist or going to the ER.
- Evidence: C
- Note: In Canada, you have a legal right to access your medical records. Many provinces now offer online portals (e.g., MyHealth in Ontario/Alberta) where you can view your own lab results directly.

### 3. Claim medical expenses on your tax return
- Cost: Time spent organizing receipts.
- Plain language: If you have significant out-of-pocket medical expenses (prescription drugs, dental work, travel for medical care, private health insurance premiums), you can claim them as a non-refundable tax credit.
- Payoff: Reduces your tax bill.
- Evidence: A (Tax Law)
- Source: CRA. Lines 33099 and 33199 – Eligible medical expenses.
- Note: You can only claim the portion that exceeds 3% of your net income (or a set maximum, whichever is less). Claim all eligible expenses for you, your spouse, and your dependents on the tax return of the spouse with the *lower* net income to maximize the benefit.

### 4. Look into provincial drug assistance programs
- Cost: Zero to apply.
- Plain language: If your medication costs are destroying your budget and you don't have private insurance (or it maxes out), every province has a catastrophic drug coverage program (e.g., Trillium in Ontario, Fair PharmaCare in BC).
- Payoff: Caps your annual out-of-pocket prescription drug costs at a percentage of your household income (usually 3–4%).
- Evidence: A (Provincial Policy)
- Source: Provincial Ministry of Health websites.
- Note: You must apply; it is not automatic. The deductible resets annually.

### 5. Take your medication exactly as prescribed (Adherence)
- Cost: Price of the medication.
- Plain language: Roughly 50% of people with chronic diseases (like high blood pressure or diabetes) stop taking their medication within a year. This is a primary driver of preventable heart attacks, strokes, and hospitalizations.
- Payoff: Prevents disease progression and early death.
- Evidence: A
- Source: WHO. Adherence to long-term therapies: evidence for action.
- Note: If you stop because of side effects, tell your doctor. There are almost always alternative medications. If you stop because of cost, use the provincial programs mentioned above or talk to your pharmacist.

### 6. Do not stop antidepressants or corticosteroids cold turkey
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Suddenly stopping SSRIs/SNRIs (antidepressants) causes severe discontinuation syndrome (brain zaps, vertigo, intense anxiety). Suddenly stopping long-term corticosteroids (like prednisone) can cause a life-threatening adrenal crisis.
- Payoff: Prevents severe withdrawal symptoms and medical emergencies.
- Evidence: A
- Source: CMAJ. Antidepressant discontinuation syndrome.
- Note: Always taper off these medications slowly under the guidance of a doctor or pharmacist.
