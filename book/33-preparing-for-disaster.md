# 33. Preparing for Disaster

> Canada is vast, and depending on where you live, you may face wildfires, floods, blizzards, or earthquakes. When infrastructure fails, you are on your own for the first 72 hours. This chapter covers realistic preparedness.

---

### 1. Build a 72-Hour Emergency Kit
- Cost: ~$100–$200.
- Plain language: In a major disaster (like an ice storm that takes down the power grid), government emergency services will be overwhelmed. You must be able to survive independently for 3 days.
- Payoff: Prevents dehydration, starvation, and hypothermia during a crisis.
- Evidence: A (Public Safety Canada)
- Source: GetPrepared.gc.ca.
- Note: Core components: 4 litres of water per person per day, non-perishable food (and a manual can opener), a crank/battery radio, flashlight, extra batteries, a first aid kit, and cash (ATMs won't work without power).

### 2. Have an Evacuation "Go Bag" ready
- Cost: Minimal.
- Plain language: If a wildfire is approaching your town (as in Fort McMurray or Lytton), you may have exactly 10 minutes to leave your house forever. Have a small bag packed and ready near the door during fire/flood season.
- Payoff: Ensures you escape with your life and your most critical documents.
- Evidence: A (Emergency Management)
- Note: Keep copies of your passport, insurance policies, and prescriptions in this bag, along with a change of clothes, phone chargers, and N95 masks (for smoke).

### 3. Know your specific regional risks
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Preparedness depends on geography. 
  - **BC Coast**: Earthquakes (secure heavy furniture to studs).
  - **Interior BC/Alberta**: Wildfires (clear brush away from your house - FireSmart Canada).
  - **Prairies**: Blizzards and extreme cold (keep a winter survival kit in your car).
  - **Ontario/Quebec/Maritimes**: Floods and Ice Storms (install a backwater valve; have a generator).
- Payoff: Targeted preparation saves money and lives.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Public Safety Canada. Regional risks.

### 4. Check if you live in a flood plain and check your insurance
- Cost: Zero to check.
- Plain language: Standard home insurance in Canada does NOT automatically cover "overland flooding" (water entering from a river overflowing or heavy rain). You usually have to buy this as extra coverage.
- Payoff: Prevents financial ruin if your home is destroyed by a flood.
- Evidence: A (Insurance Bureau of Canada)
- Note: Check the flood maps for your municipality. If you are in a high-risk zone, the insurance rider might be expensive, but you need it.

### 5. Keep your car gas tank at least half full during winter
- Cost: Zero (you're paying for the gas eventually anyway).
- Plain language: If a sudden blizzard hits and you are stuck in traffic on a highway for 12 hours, your car's engine is the only thing keeping you from freezing to death. If you run out of gas, you have no heat.
- Payoff: Prevents hypothermia.
- Evidence: B
- Note: Keep a blanket, a small shovel, and some high-calorie snacks in the trunk from November to April.

### 6. Have a family communications plan
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: During a disaster, cell towers are often overloaded or lose power. Have a designated out-of-province contact that everyone in your family will call or text to check in. (A text message requires far less bandwidth than a voice call and is more likely to go through).
- Payoff: Prevents panic when you can't reach your spouse who is at work across town.
- Evidence: C (Emergency Management best practices)
