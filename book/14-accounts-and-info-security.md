# 14. Accounts & Information Security

> Your digital identity holds the keys to your money and reputation. This chapter covers the high-yield security practices that actually matter.

---

### 1. Turn on Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) everywhere, starting with your email
- Cost: Free. Takes 10 minutes.
- Plain language: Passwords get stolen in data breaches constantly. MFA (or 2FA) means a hacker needs your password AND your phone to get in. If you only secure one thing, secure your primary email account (Gmail/Outlook) — because that's where password resets for all your other accounts go.
- Payoff: Microsoft reports that enabling MFA blocks 99.9% of automated account compromise attacks.
- Evidence: A (Cybersecurity industry consensus)
- Note: Authenticator apps (like Google Authenticator, Authy) are safer than receiving SMS text messages, as SMS can be intercepted via SIM-swapping.

### 2. Use a Password Manager
- Cost: Free (e.g., Bitwarden) to $40/year (e.g., 1Password).
- Plain language: Humans cannot memorize 50 different, complex passwords. If you reuse the same password, a breach at a random forum gives hackers the password to your bank. A password manager generates and remembers a unique, complex password for every site.
- Payoff: Prevents credential stuffing attacks. You only have to remember one strong master password.
- Evidence: A
- Note: Apple's built-in Keychain and Google Chrome's password manager are also acceptable free alternatives, though dedicated apps often offer better cross-platform support.

### 3. Freeze your credit if your identity is stolen
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: If your wallet is stolen or you fall for a phishing scam giving away your SIN, criminals will try to open credit cards in your name. You can contact Equifax and TransUnion Canada to place a "fraud alert" or "credit freeze" on your file, which prevents lenders from issuing new credit without contacting you first.
- Payoff: Stops identity thieves from racking up tens of thousands of dollars in debt under your name.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada.
- Note: You must contact *both* Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada, as they operate separately.

### 4. Never give a 2FA code to someone calling you
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: If your "bank" or "telecom provider" calls you, says there is fraud on your account, and tells you they just texted you a 6-digit verification code that you need to read back to them — hang up. It's a scammer trying to log into your account; they triggered the code and need you to give it to them to bypass the security.
- Payoff: Prevents immediate account takeover.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre.
- Note: No legitimate company will call you and ask you to read back a 2FA code.

### 5. Set up a PIN with your telecom provider to prevent SIM swapping
- Cost: Free. Takes 5 minutes.
- Plain language: A scammer can call Rogers/Bell/Telus, pretend to be you, and ask to port your phone number to a new SIM card. Once they have your phone number, they can bypass SMS-based 2FA on your bank accounts. Ask your provider to add a security PIN to your account that must be provided before any changes are made.
- Payoff: Blocks SIM-swap attacks.
- Evidence: B
- Note: Use an Authenticator app instead of SMS for 2FA wherever possible, as it completely negates the SIM-swap threat.

### 6. Know how to wipe your phone remotely
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: Ensure "Find My iPhone" (Apple) or "Find My Device" (Android) is turned on. If your phone is stolen, you can log into a computer and remotely erase all data on the device.
- Payoff: Prevents thieves from accessing your emails, photos, and banking apps.
- Evidence: A
- Note: Make sure your phone also requires a strong passcode or biometric (FaceID/fingerprint) to unlock.
