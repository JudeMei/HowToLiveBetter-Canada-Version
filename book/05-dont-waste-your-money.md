# 5. Don't Waste Your Money

> Most people lose money not on big investments, but on high fees, high interest, depreciation, and buying things they don't need. This chapter covers the highest-yield financial moves for Canadians.

---

### 1. Pay off high-interest debt immediately (credit cards, payday loans)
- Cost: Requires available cash, but pays guaranteed high returns.
- Plain language: A credit card charges 20% interest. A payday loan charges up to 390% (annualized). Paying off a 20% credit card is equivalent to finding a guaranteed, tax-free investment that pays 20% — which doesn't exist anywhere else.
- Payoff: Massive financial savings. If you carry a $5,000 balance at 19.99% and only make the minimum payment, it will take decades to pay off and cost thousands in interest.
- Evidence: A (mathematical certainty)
- Source: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada. Credit cards.
- Note: If you are drowning in debt, speak to a non-profit credit counsellor (e.g., Credit Counselling Canada) or a Licensed Insolvency Trustee. Do not use for-profit "debt settlement" companies.

### 2. Take the free money: Employer RRSP match
- Cost: You must contribute a percentage of your salary (usually 3–5%).
- Plain language: If your employer offers an RRSP match (e.g., they match 100% of your contributions up to 5% of your salary), it is an instant 100% return on your money. Never leave this on the table.
- Payoff: Mathematical certainty. No other investment guarantees an immediate 50% or 100% return.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada. Retirement planning.
- Note: Contribute exactly enough to get the maximum match. If you have high-interest debt, get the match first, then put the rest toward the debt.

### 3. Take the free money: RESP (Canada Education Savings Grant)
- Cost: Requires contributing to a child's Registered Education Savings Plan.
- Plain language: The federal government gives a 20% match on the first $2,500 you contribute to a child's RESP each year (up to $500/year, lifetime max $7,200 per child). Low-income families get even more (Canada Learning Bond).
- Payoff: An instant 20% guaranteed return on your investment, plus tax-sheltered growth.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Employment and Social Development Canada. Canada Education Savings Grant.
- Note: You can catch up on one missed year at a time (contribute $5,000 to get $1,000 grant). The child must use it for qualifying post-secondary education, but the definition is very broad (university, college, trade school).

### 4. Invest in low-cost, broadly diversified index funds or ETFs
- Cost: MER (Management Expense Ratio) of 0.1% to 0.25%, instead of the 2.0%+ charged by typical Canadian mutual funds.
- Plain language: Canadian bank mutual funds have some of the highest fees in the world (~2% per year). A 2% fee eats about 40% of your total returns over 25 years. Buy a low-cost all-in-one ETF (like VEQT or XEQT) instead.
- Payoff: Over a 25-year period, investing $500/month at a 7% return before fees: With a 2% fee, you end up with ~$290,000. With a 0.2% fee, you end up with ~$390,000. You save $100,000 just by avoiding high fees.
- Evidence: A
- Source: SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active) Canada Scorecard. Consistently shows that over 10-year periods, 80-90%+ of active Canadian equity fund managers underperform the benchmark index.
- Note: Use a discount brokerage (e.g., Wealthsimple, Questrade) to buy ETFs commission-free, or a robo-advisor if you want it fully automated.

### 5. Use your TFSA before your RRSP (for most people)
- Cost: Free to open.
- Plain language: A Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) means you never pay tax on the growth or withdrawals. An RRSP gives you a tax refund now, but you pay tax on the withdrawals in retirement. If you make under ~$80,000, max out the TFSA first.
- Payoff: Optimal tax efficiency. If you are in a lower tax bracket now than you will be in retirement (or the same), the TFSA wins mathematically. RRSPs are best when you are in a high tax bracket now and expect to be in a lower one in retirement.
- Evidence: A (tax mathematics)
- Source: Canada Revenue Agency. The TFSA vs RRSP math is standard financial planning practice in Canada.
- Note: A TFSA is not just a savings account — you can hold stocks, ETFs, and bonds inside it. Don't leave your TFSA sitting in cash earning 1% while inflation is 3%. (Exception: if your employer matches RRSP contributions, do that first).

### 6. If you're a first-time home buyer, use the FHSA
- Cost: Free to open.
- Plain language: The First Home Savings Account (FHSA) is the best of both worlds: tax-deductible contributions (like an RRSP) and tax-free withdrawals when you buy a home (like a TFSA).
- Payoff: If you contribute the max ($8,000/year up to $40,000 lifetime), you reduce your taxable income by that amount, get a tax refund, and never pay tax on the investment growth when you buy a qualifying home.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Canada Revenue Agency. First Home Savings Account (FHSA).
- Note: Introduced in 2023. You can carry forward up to $8,000 of unused contribution room to the next year. If you don't buy a home within 15 years, you can transfer it tax-free to your RRSP.

### 7. Never buy a new car if you care about money (buy 3–5 years old)
- Cost: Saves tens of thousands of dollars in depreciation.
- Plain language: A new car loses 20–30% of its value the second you drive it off the lot, and ~50% of its value in the first 4 years. Buy a gently used 4-year-old car; someone else has paid for the steepest depreciation.
- Payoff: Financial savings.
- Evidence: A (market data)
- Source: Canadian Black Book vehicle depreciation data.
- Note: Exception: the used car market went crazy during the pandemic (2020–2023), sometimes making new cars a better deal due to interest rates, but it is returning to historical norms. Always check insurance rates before buying — some models are vastly more expensive to insure.

### 8. Don't buy extended warranties on electronics or appliances
- Cost: Saves 10–20% on the purchase price.
- Plain language: Extended warranties are highly profitable for retailers because the expected payout is much lower than the cost. Self-insure for small items.
- Payoff: You save the premium. If one item breaks, the money you saved by not buying warranties on your other items will pay for the replacement.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Consumer Reports consistently advises against extended warranties for electronics and appliances.
- Note: Many premium credit cards in Canada automatically double the manufacturer's warranty for free. Check your card's benefits guide.

### 9. Cancel subscriptions you don't use regularly
- Cost: Saves $10–$50+ per month. Takes 15 minutes.
- Plain language: "Subscription creep" drains your bank account. Companies rely on you being too lazy to cancel.
- Payoff: Immediate cash flow improvement.
- Evidence: C
- Note: Go through your last two credit card statements and cancel any subscription you haven't actively used in the last 30 days. You can always resubscribe later if you miss it.

### 10. Avoid MLMs (Multi-Level Marketing) entirely
- Cost: Saves money, time, and relationships.
- Plain language: MLMs (Avon, Amway, Mary Kay, doTERRA, Beachbody, etc.) are structured so that 99% of participants lose money. The primary customer is the distributor, not the public.
- Payoff: Protects you from financial loss and alienating your friends and family.
- Evidence: A
- Source: FTC (Federal Trade Commission, US) study of 350 MLMs found that 99% of participants lose money after accounting for expenses.
- Note: If recruitment is a major part of how you get paid, it's an MLM. Run away.

### 11. Do not invest in cryptocurrency unless you consider it gambling
- Cost: Prevents total loss of capital.
- Plain language: Cryptocurrencies are highly volatile, speculative assets with no intrinsic value, no dividend yield, and no earnings. They are frequently subject to fraud, exchange collapses (e.g., FTX, QuadrigaCX), and regulatory crackdowns.
- Payoff: Avoids catastrophic losses. The vast majority of retail investors who buy crypto during hype cycles lose money.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Bank of Canada. Staff Analytical Notes on Cryptoasset ownership in Canada; numerous securities commission warnings.
- Note: If you must buy it, limit it to 1–5% of your portfolio and treat it as speculative gambling money you can afford to lose completely. Cryptocurrencies are not protected by CDIC or CIPF.
