# 1. Don't Die Early

> The cheapest way to live longer is to stop dying from things that are entirely preventable. This chapter covers the leading causes of accidental and premature death in Canada and what costs almost nothing to avoid them.

Items are sorted by value-for-effort: the ones that cost the least and save the most lives go first.

---

### 1. Install and maintain smoke detectors on every floor and outside every sleeping area
- Cost: $10–$30 per detector. Five minutes to install. Test monthly, replace batteries yearly, replace unit every 10 years.
- Plain language: Having working smoke detectors cuts your chance of dying in a house fire roughly in half.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis of observational studies found that homes with working smoke alarms had 50% lower fire death rates compared to homes without (OR ~0.50). The National Fire Protection Association reports that three out of five home fire deaths occur in homes with no smoke alarms or non-working alarms.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Ahrens M. (2021). Smoke Alarms in US Home Fires. NFPA Research. https://www.nfpa.org/education-and-research/research/nfpa-research/fire-statistical-reports/smoke-alarms-in-us-home-fires; Statistics Canada, Table 35-10-0191-01.
- Note: Required by law in every province and territory. Many fire departments give them away free. Interconnected alarms (when one goes off, they all go off) are significantly more effective. Replace any alarm older than 10 years — they stop working reliably.

### 2. Install a carbon monoxide (CO) detector on every floor
- Cost: $20–$50 per detector. Mandatory in Ontario, Alberta, and several other provinces if you have any fuel-burning appliance or attached garage.
- Plain language: Carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless. It kills about 300 Canadians per year. A $30 detector is the only way to know it's there before you lose consciousness.
- Payoff: CO poisoning causes approximately 300 deaths per year in Canada. Detectors provide early warning; without them, occupants have no way to detect CO before symptoms of poisoning appear. Observational data show that homes with CO detectors have near-zero rates of CO death.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Health Canada. Carbon Monoxide. https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/indoor-air-contaminants/carbon-monoxide.html; CMAJ 2012; 184(17): E902–E912.
- Note: Required by building codes in most provinces. Especially critical if you have a natural gas furnace, wood stove, or attached garage. Never run a generator or barbecue indoors — this is the #1 cause of mass CO poisoning events in Canada.

### 3. Keep a naloxone kit at home and learn how to use it (2 minutes)
- Cost: Free at any pharmacy in Canada, no prescription needed. Takes 2 minutes to learn: the pharmacist shows you.
- Plain language: An opioid overdose kills in minutes. Naloxone reverses it temporarily and buys time for paramedics. There are no significant side effects if given to someone who isn't actually overdosing.
- Payoff: Opioid-related deaths in Canada exceeded 42,000 from January 2016 to June 2023. Naloxone administered by bystanders has a success rate of approximately 75–100% in reversing respiratory depression. Time to treatment is the primary predictor of survival. A systematic review found bystander naloxone distribution programs reduced overdose mortality in communities by 27–46%.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Walley AY et al. (2013). Opioid overdose rates and implementation of overdose education and nasal naloxone distribution in Massachusetts: interrupted time series analysis. BMJ. 346:f174. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.f174; Special Advisory Committee on the Epidemic of Opioid Overdoses. Opioid and Stimulant-related Harms in Canada. https://health-infobase.canada.ca/substance-related-harms/opioids-stimulants/
- Note: You don't need to be at risk yourself. If you ever encounter someone unconscious from an unknown cause, naloxone is safe to administer — if they're not overdosing, it simply does nothing. Kit comes with instructions. Call 911 immediately after administering.

### 4. Get your recommended vaccines on schedule
- Cost: Free under provincial immunization programs for recommended vaccines. Minor time for the appointment.
- Plain language: Vaccines are among the most cost-effective medical interventions ever developed. Influenza alone kills 3,500 Canadians in a typical year; the flu shot reduces your risk of hospitalization by 40–60%.
- Payoff: The WHO estimates vaccines prevent 3.5–5 million deaths globally per year. Influenza vaccination reduces hospitalizations by 40–60% in healthy adults during seasons when the vaccine matches circulating strains (Cochrane review). Pneumococcal vaccine reduces invasive pneumococcal disease by 45–75% in adults 65+. Shingles vaccine (Shingrix) has 97.2% efficacy in adults 50+ (RCT, ZOE-50 trial).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Demicheli V et al. (2018). Vaccines for preventing influenza in healthy adults. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001269.pub6; Lal H et al. (2015). Efficacy of an adjuvanted herpes zoster subunit vaccine in older adults. NEJM. 372:2087-2096. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1501184
- Note: NACI (National Advisory Committee on Immunization) publishes the Canadian Immunization Guide — check it for your age group's schedule. COVID-19, influenza (annual), shingles (50+), pneumococcal (65+), Tdap booster (every 10 years) are the most commonly missed in adults. Provincial programs cover costs differently — most are free.

### 5. Wear a seatbelt every time you're in a vehicle
- Cost: Zero. Three seconds.
- Plain language: Seatbelts reduce your chance of dying in a car crash by about 45%.
- Payoff: The US NHTSA estimates seatbelts reduce fatal injury risk by 45% for front-seat passenger car occupants and 60% for light truck occupants. Transport Canada reports that unbelted occupants account for approximately 30% of motor vehicle fatalities despite belt-wearing rates above 95%.
- Evidence: A
- Source: NHTSA (2017). Lives Saved in 2016 by Restraint Use and Minimum-Drinking-Age Laws. DOT HS 812 454; Transport Canada. Canadian Motor Vehicle Traffic Collision Statistics. https://tc.canada.ca/en/road-transportation/statistics-data/canadian-motor-vehicle-traffic-collision-statistics
- Note: Includes rear seats. Rear-seat passengers without belts are 8× more likely to die. Children under 40 lbs must use a car seat; under 80 lbs / 4'9" use a booster. Provincial law varies on age/weight thresholds — check your province.

### 6. Never drive impaired (alcohol, cannabis, or other substances)
- Cost: Zero — just plan ahead: designated driver, taxi, transit, or crash at a friend's place.
- Plain language: Impaired driving is the leading criminal cause of death in Canada. A first-offence DUI conviction carries a minimum $1,000 fine, a 1-year driving prohibition, and a criminal record that can block you from entering the US for life.
- Payoff: Approximately 1,500 Canadians are killed annually in impaired driving crashes. The Criminal Code (s. 320.14) sets the legal BAC limit at 0.08 (80 mg/dL), but most provinces impose administrative penalties (licence suspension, vehicle impoundment) starting at 0.05. For cannabis, any detectable THC above 2 ng/mL results in a summary conviction; above 5 ng/mL is a hybrid offence.
- Evidence: A
- Source: MADD Canada. (2022). Impaired Driving Statistics. https://madd.ca/pages/impaired-driving/overview/statistics/; Criminal Code of Canada, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-46, Part VIII.1.
- Note: A criminal record for impaired driving is permanent unless you get a record suspension (pardon) — minimum 5-year wait after completing your sentence. US Customs can refuse entry to anyone with a Canadian DUI conviction, even decades later. Ride-sharing apps have made this the most preventable category of death.

### 7. Learn Hands-Only CPR (takes 1 minute to learn the basics)
- Cost: Free. Watch a 1-minute video or take a free community course (many offered by Heart & Stroke Foundation, Red Cross, St. John Ambulance).
- Plain language: When someone's heart stops, every minute without CPR lowers their survival chance by about 10%. Bystander CPR roughly doubles or triples the chance of survival.
- Payoff: In Canada, approximately 35,000 cardiac arrests occur outside hospitals each year. Survival rate without bystander CPR: ~5%. With bystander CPR: ~12–15%. With bystander CPR + AED: ~30–40%. A meta-analysis of 79 studies found bystander CPR was associated with a 2-fold increase in survival to hospital discharge (OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.81–2.45).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Sasson C et al. (2010). Predictors of survival from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Circ Cardiovasc Qual Outcomes. 3(1):63-81. https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCOUTCOMES.109.889576; Heart & Stroke Foundation of Canada. 2023 Annual Report on Resuscitation.
- Note: Hands-Only CPR means push hard and fast in the centre of the chest (100–120 compressions per minute, about the tempo of "Stayin' Alive"). No mouth-to-mouth needed. Call 911 first — the dispatcher will coach you through it. Don't worry about cracking ribs — that's normal and the person will die without compressions.

### 8. Know where AEDs are in your workplace, gym, and community
- Cost: Zero. Just look for the signs — they're red or green heart-shaped signs on walls. Takes 30 seconds to note their location.
- Plain language: Using an AED within the first 3–5 minutes of cardiac arrest increases survival to 50–70%. The machine tells you exactly what to do — you cannot shock someone who doesn't need it.
- Payoff: AED use before ambulance arrival is associated with survival rates of 50–70%, compared to 5–12% without. AEDs are designed for untrained bystanders — they analyze the heart rhythm and will not deliver a shock unless one is needed.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Berdowski J et al. (2010). Global incidences of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and survival rates: Systematic review of 67 prospective studies. Resuscitation. 81(11):1479-1487. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2010.08.006
- Note: All provinces have Good Samaritan legislation protecting bystanders who use AEDs in good faith. Many provinces mandate AEDs in public buildings. Ask your workplace if they have one — many do, but no one knows where it is.

### 9. Get screened for colorectal cancer starting at age 50 (or 45 if high-risk)
- Cost: Free under provincial health insurance. FIT (fecal immunochemical test) is a simple at-home stool test every 2 years; colonoscopy every 10 years if FIT is positive.
- Plain language: Colorectal cancer is the 2nd leading cause of cancer death in Canada. Screening catches it early when it's 90% curable; late detection drops survival to 14%.
- Payoff: A Cochrane review of 4 RCTs (n=320,000+) found that fecal occult blood testing (the older version of FIT) reduced colorectal cancer mortality by 16% (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.78–0.90). Colonoscopy with polypectomy reduces CRC incidence by 40–60% by removing precancerous polyps. 5-year survival: localized (Stage I) ~91%; distant (Stage IV) ~14%.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Hewitson P et al. (2008). Cochrane systematic review of colorectal cancer screening using the fecal occult blood test (Hemoccult). Am J Gastroenterol. 103(6):1541-9. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1572-0241.2008.01875.x; Canadian Cancer Society. Colorectal cancer statistics. https://cancer.ca/en/cancer-information/cancer-types/colorectal/statistics
- Note: The Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care recommends starting at 50 for average-risk individuals. If you have a first-degree relative with CRC, talk to your doctor about starting at 40 or earlier. FIT kits are available from your family doctor or provincial screening program.

### 10. Get screened for cervical cancer (Pap test or HPV test) as recommended
- Cost: Free under provincial health insurance. Pap test every 3 years (ages 25–69) or HPV primary screening every 5 years (varies by province).
- Plain language: Cervical cancer screening has reduced cervical cancer deaths by more than 70% since its introduction. Almost all cervical cancer is preventable with screening + HPV vaccination.
- Payoff: Cervical cancer incidence and mortality have declined by over 70% in countries with organized screening programs (Peto J et al., 2004). HPV vaccination (given at age 9–14) reduces HPV 16/18 infections by over 90% and is expected to reduce cervical cancer incidence by 80–90% in vaccinated cohorts over time.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Peto J et al. (2004). The cervical cancer epidemic that screening has prevented in the UK. Lancet. 364(9430):249-56. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(04)16674-9; Lei J et al. (2020). HPV Vaccination and the Risk of Invasive Cervical Cancer. NEJM. 383:1340-1348. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1917338
- Note: Province-funded HPV vaccines (Gardasil 9) are free for school-age children in all provinces. Adults who missed it can get it — cost is ~$200–$600 for the series if not covered. Trans men and non-binary people with a cervix should also be screened.

### 11. Get mammography screening as recommended (ages 50–74)
- Cost: Free under provincial screening programs. Mammogram every 2 years.
- Plain language: Regular mammography screening reduces breast cancer death by about 20% for women aged 50–69.
- Payoff: A Cochrane review of 11 RCTs (n=600,000+) found that mammography screening reduced breast cancer mortality by approximately 20% (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.73–0.89) in women aged 50–69. Canadian Breast Cancer Foundation data show 5-year survival is 99% for Stage I vs. 27% for Stage IV.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Gøtzsche PC, Jørgensen KJ. (2013). Screening for breast cancer with mammography. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. (6):CD001877. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001877.pub5; Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care (2024). Breast Cancer Screening Guidelines.
- Note: Controversy. The Canadian Task Force updated guidelines in 2024 — check the current recommendation for your age group. Overdiagnosis (detecting cancers that would never have caused harm) is a real concern. Discuss benefits and harms with your doctor.

### 12. Get lung cancer screening if you qualify (low-dose CT)
- Cost: Free in provinces with organized screening programs (expanding across Canada). Eligible: age 50–80, 20+ pack-year smoking history, current or quit within 15 years.
- Plain language: Low-dose CT screening reduces lung cancer death by 20% in heavy smokers.
- Payoff: The NLST trial (n=53,454 heavy smokers) found low-dose CT screening reduced lung cancer mortality by 20% (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.73–0.93) compared to chest X-ray. The NELSON trial confirmed the finding (24% reduction in men).
- Evidence: A
- Source: National Lung Screening Trial Research Team. (2011). Reduced lung-cancer mortality with low-dose computed tomographic screening. NEJM. 365:395-409. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1102873; de Koning HJ et al. (2020). Reduced Lung-Cancer Mortality with Volume CT Screening in a Randomized Trial. NEJM. 382:503-513. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1911793
- Note: Only beneficial for heavy smokers meeting criteria. False positives are common (~25% of screened individuals get flagged, most are benign). Check if your province has a lung cancer screening program.

### 13. Don't smoke indoors or near children
- Cost: Zero. Step outside.
- Plain language: Secondhand smoke increases a child's risk of SIDS by 2×, ear infections by 50%, and asthma attacks by 20–30%. There is no safe level of secondhand smoke exposure.
- Payoff: The US Surgeon General concluded there is no safe level of exposure to secondhand smoke. Children exposed to secondhand smoke have 1.5–2× higher risk of SIDS (meta-analysis, OR 1.94 for maternal smoking), 50% higher risk of middle ear disease, and 20–30% higher risk of acute lower respiratory infections.
- Evidence: A
- Source: US DHHS. (2006). The Health Consequences of Involuntary Exposure to Tobacco Smoke: A Report of the Surgeon General; Zhang K, Wang X. (2013). Maternal smoking and increased risk of sudden infant death syndrome: a meta-analysis. Leg Med. 15(3):115-21. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.legalmed.2012.10.007
- Note: Smoking is banned in all indoor public places, workplaces, and vehicles with minors present across Canada (provincial laws). Thirdhand smoke (residue on surfaces) also harms children.

### 14. Store firearms locked, unloaded, with ammunition stored separately
- Cost: Gun safe or trigger lock: $30–$500. Required by law.
- Plain language: Firearms are involved in roughly 75% of suicides among firearms owners. Secure storage reduces the risk of suicide and accidental death, especially among youth.
- Payoff: A systematic review found that households with locked firearms had 73% lower risk of firearm suicide and unintentional death among youth (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.12–0.60). The Firearms Act requires that all firearms be stored unloaded, locked (trigger lock or locked container), and with ammunition stored separately or locked.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Grossman DC et al. (2005). Gun storage practices and risk of youth suicide and unintentional firearm injuries. JAMA. 293(6):707-14. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.293.6.707; Firearms Act, S.C. 1995, c. 39; Storage, Display, Transportation and Handling of Firearms by Individuals Regulations, SOR/98-209.
- Note: Non-compliance is a criminal offence (Criminal Code s. 86). If someone in your household is in crisis, temporarily remove firearms or have a trusted person store them.

### 15. Use winter tires from November to April (mandatory in Quebec; essential everywhere else)
- Cost: $400–$1,200 for a set, lasting 4–5 winters. Many shops offer free tire storage.
- Plain language: Winter tires reduce your stopping distance on ice by up to 25% and significantly reduce collision risk.
- Payoff: Transport Canada data show winter tires reduce stopping distance by up to 25% on ice compared to all-season tires. A Quebec study found that making winter tires mandatory reduced winter collisions by approximately 5% and injuries by 3%.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Transport Canada. (2019). Winter Driving — Tires. https://tc.canada.ca/en/road-transportation/winter-driving; Desapriya E et al. (2014). Impact of mandatory winter tire regulation on collision rates in Quebec. Accid Anal Prev. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aap.2014.04.010
- Note: Mandatory in Quebec (Dec 1 – Mar 15) and BC highways (Oct 1 – Apr 30 on many routes). Insurance companies in some provinces offer discounts for winter tires. "All-weather" tires (with the snowflake-and-mountain symbol) meet the legal requirement but perform worse than dedicated winter tires.

### 16. Test your home for radon
- Cost: $30–$50 for a long-term test kit (Health Canada recommends 3+ months, ideally during heating season). Free test kits sometimes available through provincial programs.
- Plain language: Radon is the #1 cause of lung cancer in non-smokers in Canada. About 7% of Canadian homes have radon levels above the guideline — a $40 test tells you.
- Payoff: Radon causes approximately 3,200 lung cancer deaths per year in Canada (Health Canada). It is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking. Health Canada's action level is 200 Bq/m³. A pooled analysis of 13 European case-control studies found a 16% increase in lung cancer risk per 100 Bq/m³ increase in residential radon concentration (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.05–1.31).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Darby S et al. (2005). Radon in homes and risk of lung cancer: collaborative analysis of individual data from 13 European case-control studies. BMJ. 330(7485):223. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38308.477650.63; Health Canada. Radon — Another Reason to Quit. https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environmental-workplace-health/radiation/radon.html
- Note: If your test result is above 200 Bq/m³, hire a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional. Typical mitigation cost: $2,000–$3,500. The system (sub-slab depressurization) lasts decades.

### 17. Never leave children or pets in a parked car, even for a few minutes
- Cost: Zero. Take them with you.
- Plain language: A car's interior can reach 50°C (122°F) in 20 minutes on a 25°C day. Children die from vehicular heatstroke in under an hour.
- Payoff: On average, 37 children die of vehicular heatstroke per year in the US (Canada tracks fewer cases but proportional risk applies). A child's body temperature rises 3–5 times faster than an adult's. Core body temperature of 40°C (104°F) causes heat stroke; 41.7°C (107°F) can be fatal.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Null J. (2023). Heatstroke Deaths of Children in Vehicles. Department of Meteorology and Climate Science, San Jose State University. https://www.noheatstroke.org/; McLaren C et al. (2005). Heat stress from enclosed vehicles: moderate ambient temperatures cause significant temperature rise in enclosed vehicles. Pediatrics. 116(1):e109-12. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2004-2368
- Note: If you see a child or animal in a hot car and they appear in distress, call 911 immediately. Good Samaritan laws in most provinces protect you if you break the window.

### 18. Keep medications and household chemicals out of children's reach
- Cost: $10–$20 for childproof locks on cabinets. Store items above counter height.
- Plain language: Poisoning is the 3rd leading cause of injury death in Canadian children under 5. One bottle of adult iron supplements or a grandparent's heart medication can kill a toddler.
- Payoff: Canadian Hospitals Injury Reporting and Prevention Program data show that unintentional poisoning accounts for roughly 8,000 ER visits per year for children under 5 in Canada. Locked storage reduces access and is the primary prevention measure recommended by Parachute Canada and poison control centres.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Parachute Canada. Poisoning Prevention. https://parachute.ca/en/injury-topic/poisoning/; Health Canada. Consumer Product Safety. https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/consumer-product-safety.html
- Note: Poison Control: call 1-800-268-9017 (Ontario) or check your province's number. Cannabis edibles are a growing cause of childhood poisoning — store in locked containers. Laundry pods are especially dangerous for toddlers.

### 19. Install and check your home's ground fault circuit interrupters (GFCIs)
- Cost: $15–$25 per outlet if retrofitting; already required by code in bathrooms, kitchens, and outdoor outlets in new construction.
- Plain language: GFCIs cut power in milliseconds when they detect current leaking through a person — they prevent electrocution.
- Payoff: The Canadian Electrical Code requires GFCIs in wet locations. CPSC data show GFCIs have reduced electrocution deaths in the US by approximately 80% since becoming mandatory in new construction. Estimated to prevent ~300 deaths and 7,000 injuries per year in North America.
- Evidence: B
- Source: CPSC. Ground-Fault Circuit Interrupters (GFCIs) Fact Sheet. https://www.cpsc.gov/safety-education/safety-guides/electrical/ground-fault-circuit-interrupters-gfcis; Canadian Electrical Code, CSA C22.1.
- Note: Test your GFCIs monthly by pressing the "Test" button. If they don't trip and reset, replace them. Older homes may not have GFCIs in all required locations — retrofitting is inexpensive and may be required when selling.

### 20. Wear a helmet when cycling, skiing, snowboarding, or skating
- Cost: $20–$100 for a cycling helmet. Mandatory for all ages in some provinces (BC, Nova Scotia, PEI, New Brunswick, Alberta under 18).
- Plain language: Helmets reduce the risk of serious head injury by about 60% and brain injury by about 53%.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis of 40 studies found that helmet use was associated with a 51% reduction in head injury (OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.42–0.57), a 69% reduction in serious head injury (OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.23–0.42), and a 33% reduction in facial injury.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Olivier J, Creighton P. (2017). Bicycle injuries and helmet use: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Int J Epidemiol. 46(1):278-292. https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw153
- Note: Provincial helmet laws vary — check your province. Helmets must be replaced after any impact, even if they look fine. Ski and snowboard helmets have reduced head injuries in snow sports by ~35%.

### 21. Have a household emergency preparedness kit (72-hour kit)
- Cost: $50–$200 to assemble; much of it you already have at home. Government of Canada recommends every household have one.
- Plain language: Ice storms, power outages, wildfires, and floods can leave you without water, power, or access to stores for 72+ hours. A basic kit with water, food, flashlight, batteries, first aid, and medications covers the gap.
- Payoff: Government of Canada and provincial emergency management organizations recommend every household maintain a 72-hour emergency kit. During the 2023 wildfire season, 230,000+ Canadians were evacuated. During the 2013 ice storm, 600,000+ Ontario homes lost power for up to 72 hours. Preparedness reduces injuries, deaths, and displacement.
- Evidence: C
- Source: Government of Canada. (2023). Your Emergency Preparedness Guide. https://www.getprepared.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/yprprds/index-en.aspx
- Note: Include: 4L water per person per day × 3 days, non-perishable food, manual can opener, flashlight, batteries, first aid kit, medications (7-day supply), phone charger (battery pack), copies of ID, cash, whistle, warm clothing. Update every 6 months.

### 22. Check the batteries in your smoke and CO detectors every 6 months (when clocks change)
- Cost: Zero if battery-powered detectors are already installed ($5 for batteries).
- Plain language: 25% of smoke detector failures are due to dead or missing batteries. Setting a biannual reminder (when daylight saving time changes) halves this risk.
- Payoff: NFPA data show that almost one-quarter of smoke alarm failures are due to dead or missing batteries. Regular testing and battery replacement bring the alarm back to full effectiveness.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Ahrens M. (2021). Smoke Alarms in US Home Fires. NFPA.
- Note: Many new detectors have sealed 10-year lithium batteries that don't need annual replacement. Even so, test monthly.

### 23. Learn the FAST signs of stroke (Face, Arms, Speech, Time)
- Cost: Zero. Takes 30 seconds to memorize.
- Plain language: Getting to the hospital within 3.5 hours of stroke onset allows clot-busting treatment that reduces disability by about 30%. Every minute of delay kills 1.9 million neurons.
- Payoff: tPA (tissue plasminogen activator) given within 3–4.5 hours of ischemic stroke onset reduces disability (OR for favourable outcome 1.75 when given within 3 hours). Each 15-minute reduction in door-to-needle time is associated with measurably better outcomes. FAST recognition by bystanders is the primary driver of early hospital arrival.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Emberson J et al. (2014). Effect of treatment delay, age, and stroke severity on the effects of intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase for acute ischaemic stroke: a meta-analysis of individual patient data from randomised trials. Lancet. 384(9958):1929-35. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(14)60584-5
- Note: FAST = Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call 911. New mnemonic BE-FAST adds Balance and Eyes (sudden vision loss). Call 911 even if symptoms resolve — a transient ischemic attack (TIA) is a warning of imminent stroke.

### 24. Keep a fire extinguisher in your kitchen and know how to use it (PASS)
- Cost: $20–$50 for a multipurpose ABC extinguisher.
- Plain language: Kitchen fires are the #1 cause of home fires in Canada. A fire extinguisher used in the first 30 seconds can prevent a small fire from becoming catastrophic.
- Payoff: Cooking is the leading cause of home fires in Canada (37% of all home fires). Portable fire extinguishers used by occupants contain or extinguish fires in approximately 80% of incidents where they are used (NFPA data).
- Evidence: B
- Source: Office of the Fire Commissioner, various provinces. Fire statistics; NFPA. (2022). Fire Extinguisher Use in US Fires.
- Note: PASS = Pull the pin, Aim at the base, Squeeze the handle, Sweep side to side. Never use water on a grease fire — use the extinguisher, a lid, or baking soda. Replace or service extinguishers every 5–12 years depending on type.

### 25. Don't text and drive
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Texting while driving makes a crash 23× more likely. Distracted driving now causes more road deaths in Canada than impaired driving.
- Payoff: Virginia Tech Transportation Institute found texting while driving increases crash risk 23× (OR 23.24). In Canada, distracted driving is now involved in 21–25% of fatal collisions. All provinces ban handheld device use while driving, with fines ranging from $287 (BC) to $1,000+ (Ontario for first offence) and demerit points.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Klauer SG et al. (2006). The Impact of Driver Inattention on Near-Crash/Crash Risk. DOT HS 810 594. NHTSA; TIRF (Traffic Injury Research Foundation). Distracted Driving in Canada.
- Note: Includes any handheld use — GPS, music, checking notifications. Use a dashboard mount and voice commands, or pull over. Penalties have been increasing: Ontario's 3rd offence is a 30-day licence suspension + $3,000 fine.

### 26. Fence your pool (4-sided isolation fencing with self-closing, self-latching gate)
- Cost: $1,500–$5,000 for proper pool fencing. Required by municipal bylaw in most jurisdictions.
- Plain language: Pool fencing reduces child drowning risk by 83%.
- Payoff: A Cochrane review (Thompson DC, Rivara FP, 2000) found that isolation fencing (4 sides, separating pool from house) reduces the risk of drowning for children under 5 by 83% (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.07–0.44).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Thompson DC, Rivara FP. (2000). Pool fencing for preventing drowning in children. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. (2):CD001047. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001047
- Note: 3-sided fencing (with the house forming the 4th side) is much less effective because children can access the pool through the house. Municipal bylaws set height and gap requirements. Supervise children at all times around water — fencing is a backup, not a substitute.

### 27. Put your phone number on your child's wristband or shoe when in crowds
- Cost: Zero (write with permanent marker) or $5 for a wristband.
- Plain language: A lost child who can point to a phone number on their wrist gets reunited with parents in minutes instead of hours.
- Payoff: Practical measure recommended by Canadian child safety organizations. No RCT exists (ethically impossible), but police and event organizers consistently report faster reunification when contact info is on the child.
- Evidence: C
- Source: Parachute Canada. Child Safety Tips.
- Note: Teach children your phone number as early as age 3–4. At large events, take a photo of your child that day (in case you need to show what they're wearing).

### 28. Keep the Poison Control Centre number saved in your phone
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Poison control gives you immediate, expert advice on whether a child (or adult) who swallowed something needs to go to the ER or can be managed at home. Prevents unnecessary ER visits and catches the ones that are genuine emergencies.
- Payoff: Poison centres across Canada handle approximately 200,000 calls per year. Studies show that 70–80% of poisoning exposures managed by poison control can be safely treated at home, avoiding unnecessary ER visits. Timely expert advice improves outcomes for serious exposures.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Canadian Association of Poison Control Centres. https://www.capcc.ca/
- Note: Numbers vary by province. Ontario: 1-800-268-9017. Quebec: 1-800-463-5060. BC: 1-800-567-8911. Alberta: 1-800-332-1414. Save your province's number now.
