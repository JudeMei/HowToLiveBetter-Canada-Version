# 3. Don't Waste Your Energy

> Mental and physical energy are finite resources. This chapter covers the evidence on how to protect your focus, avoid burnout, and stop bleeding energy on things that don't matter.

---

### 1. Protect your sleep environment (cool, dark, quiet)
- Cost: Minor ($20 for a sleep mask/earplugs, $50 for blackout curtains).
- Plain language: Poor sleep quality ruins your executive function, mood, and metabolism the next day. A cool, completely dark room is the most effective behavioral intervention for sleep maintenance.
- Payoff: Meta-analyses show that environmental interventions (specifically darkness and temperature control around 18–19°C) significantly improve sleep architecture (more deep sleep, fewer awakenings). Sleep deprivation impairs prefrontal cortex function (decision-making, emotional regulation) equivalent to a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Okamoto-Mizuno K, Mizuno K. (2012). Effects of thermal environment on sleep and circadian rhythm. J Physiol Anthropol. 31(1):14. https://doi.org/10.1186/1880-6805-31-14; Goel N et al. (2009). Neurobehavioral effects of sleep deprivation. Semin Neurol. 29(4):320-339.
- Note: Keep the bedroom strictly for sleep and sex. Remove screens.

### 2. Turn off non-essential notifications on your phone
- Cost: Zero. Takes 2 minutes.
- Plain language: Every notification breaks your concentration. It takes an average of 23 minutes to return to a deep state of focus after an interruption.
- Payoff: An observational study (Mark G et al., 2008, CHI) found that workers took an average of 23 minutes and 15 seconds to return to their original task after an interruption. Frequent interruptions increase stress, frustration, and perceived workload.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Mark G et al. (2008). The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress. Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems. https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072
- Note: Leave on phone calls, calendar alerts, and texts from immediate family. Turn off all social media, news, and game notifications.

### 3. Stop multitasking — do one thing at a time
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: "Multitasking" is actually task-switching. It makes you slower, more prone to errors, and exhausts your brain faster.
- Payoff: Experimental psychology shows that the human brain cannot perform two attention-demanding tasks simultaneously. "Task-switching costs" (Rubinstein JS et al., 2001) reduce productivity by up to 40% and increase error rates.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Rubinstein JS et al. (2001). Executive control of cognitive processes in task switching. J Exp Psychol Hum Percept Perform. 27(4):763-797.
- Note: Close unused browser tabs. Put your phone in another room when doing deep work.

### 4. Practice saying "no" to low-value commitments
- Cost: Brief social discomfort.
- Plain language: Every commitment is a debt on your future time and energy. Saying no protects your ability to do the things that actually matter.
- Payoff: Reduces burnout and emotional exhaustion. The psychological cost of "people-pleasing" (chronic overcommitment) correlates strongly with burnout (Maslach Burnout Inventory).
- Evidence: C
- Source: Maslach C, Leiter MP. (2016). Understanding the burnout experience: recent research and its implications for psychiatry. World Psychiatry. 15(2):103-111.
- Note: Use a script: "Thank you for thinking of me, but I don't have the capacity to take this on right now." You don't need to provide a detailed excuse.

### 5. Automate or delegate recurring low-value decisions
- Cost: Minor initial setup time.
- Plain language: Decision fatigue is real. The more choices you make, the worse your choices become. Automate bills, meals, and clothing choices.
- Payoff: Eliminates the cognitive load of trivial decisions. Experimental evidence (Baumeister RF et al., 1998) suggests self-control and decision-making draw on a limited resource (ego depletion), though the effect size is debated. Regardless of the mechanism, automating decisions saves time and reduces stress.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Baumeister RF et al. (1998). Ego depletion: is the active self a limited resource? J Pers Soc Psychol. 74(5):1252-1265.
- Note: Examples: set bills to auto-pay, eat the same breakfast every day, create a capsule wardrobe.

### 6. Manage your expectations when dealing with government or corporate bureaucracy
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Bureaucracy is designed for compliance, not customer service. Expecting it to be fast or logical will only cause you frustration.
- Payoff: Reduces psychological distress. "Administrative burden" (Herd & Moynihan, 2018) imposes psychological costs. Lowering expectations and preparing necessary documents in advance reduces friction.
- Evidence: C
- Source: Herd P, Moynihan DP. (2018). Administrative Burden: Policymaking by Other Means. Russell Sage Foundation.
- Note: When calling the CRA or Service Canada, expect to wait. Have your SIN, recent tax return, and all relevant documents in front of you before you dial. Call early in the morning for shorter wait times.
