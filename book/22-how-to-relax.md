# 22. How to Relax

> True relaxation restores your energy. "Fake" relaxation (like doomscrolling or binge drinking) depletes it further. This chapter covers evidence-based ways to recover from stress.

---

### 1. Spend time in green spaces (Nature exposure)
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: Spending at least 120 minutes a week in nature (parks, forests, beaches) is strongly associated with better self-reported health and higher psychological well-being.
- Payoff: Reduces cortisol levels (stress hormone), lowers blood pressure, and improves mood.
- Evidence: A
- Source: White MP et al. (2019). Spending at least 120 minutes a week in nature is associated with good health and wellbeing. Scientific Reports. 9:7730. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-44097-3
- Note: The 120 minutes can be done all at once or broken into smaller walks throughout the week.

### 2. Use exercise as a primary stress-reduction tool
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: Aerobic exercise is one of the most effective interventions for mild to moderate depression and anxiety. It burns off the physical byproducts of stress.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis shows exercise has a large and significant antidepressant effect.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Schuch FB et al. (2016). Exercise as a treatment for depression: A meta-analysis adjusting for publication bias. J Psychiatr Res. 77:42-51.
- Note: Find something you actually enjoy — walking, cycling, swimming, or dancing. Consistency matters more than intensity for stress relief.

### 3. Practice mindfulness or meditation for anxiety
- Cost: Free (using apps like Insight Timer) or minor subscription costs.
- Plain language: Mindfulness meditation programs have moderate evidence of improving anxiety, depression, and pain.
- Payoff: Reduces the psychological burden of stress.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Goyal M et al. (2014). Meditation programs for psychological stress and well-being: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Intern Med. 174(3):357-368.
- Note: It does not work for everyone, and it is not a replacement for medical treatment for severe clinical depression.

### 4. Maintain strong social connections
- Cost: Time and effort.
- Plain language: Loneliness is as harmful to your physical health as smoking 15 cigarettes a day. Having a few close friends you can rely on is a massive buffer against stress.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Holt-Lunstad J et al., 2010, PLoS Med) found that individuals with adequate social relationships have a 50% greater likelihood of survival compared to those with poor or insufficient social relationships.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Holt-Lunstad J et al. (2010). Social Relationships and Mortality Risk: A Meta-analytic Review. PLoS Med. 7(7): e1000316.
- Note: Quality matters more than quantity. Join clubs, volunteer, or schedule regular calls with friends.

### 5. Check your EAP (Employee Assistance Program) for free therapy
- Cost: Free (covered by your employer).
- Plain language: Most medium-to-large Canadian employers offer an EAP, which provides 3 to 6 free, confidential sessions with a therapist or counsellor.
- Payoff: Free access to professional help during a crisis.
- Evidence: B
- Note: EAPs are completely confidential; your employer does not know why you called or what you discussed.

### 6. Avoid relying on alcohol or cannabis to "wind down"
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Using substances to cope with stress creates a rebound effect. Alcohol disrupts your sleep architecture (preventing REM sleep), leaving you more exhausted and anxious the next day.
- Payoff: Prevents the development of substance use disorders and preserves sleep quality.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Ebrahim IO et al. (2013). Alcohol and sleep I: effects on normal sleep. Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research.
- Note: If you feel you *need* a drink to handle your stress, that is a warning sign. Shift to physical or psychological relaxation techniques.
