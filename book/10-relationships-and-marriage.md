# 10. Relationships and Marriage

> Research on relationship stability and the legal realities of partnering in Canada.

---

### 1. Discuss finances, debt, and child-rearing before moving in
- Cost: One difficult conversation.
- Plain language: The leading predictors of divorce are financial stress and disagreements over division of labor/childcare. You must know your partner's credit score, total debt, and expectations before combining lives.
- Payoff: Prevents legally binding yourself to someone whose financial habits will ruin you.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Dew J et al. (2012). Examining the Relationship Between Financial Issues and Divorce. Family Relations. 61(4):615-628.
- Note: In Canada, marriage legally entangles your net worth. Common-law (after a certain period) often triggers spousal support obligations even if property isn't automatically divided.

### 2. Recognize the signs of an abusive relationship early (The Power and Control Wheel)
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Physical violence is rarely the first sign. Abuse usually starts with isolation (distancing you from friends), extreme jealousy, controlling your finances, and gaslighting.
- Payoff: Escaping before it escalates to violence or homicide.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Domestic Abuse Intervention Programs (Duluth Model).
- Note: If you need help, text or call 211 to be connected to local shelters. Use an incognito browser to search for resources.

### 3. If you get married, understand the equalization of net family property
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: In most provinces (like Ontario), when you divorce, the wealth you *gained during the marriage* is split 50/50. It does not matter whose name the bank account is in, or who earned the higher salary.
- Payoff: Legal clarity.
- Evidence: A (Family Law)
- Source: Family Law Act (Ontario), s. 5.
- Note: The "matrimonial home" (the house you live in together) is treated specially in some provinces (e.g., Ontario): its full value is split 50/50, even if one person bought it before the marriage, unless there is a prenuptial agreement.

### 4. Consider a Cohabitation Agreement or Prenuptial Agreement (Marriage Contract)
- Cost: $1,500–$3,000 for independent legal advice.
- Plain language: A prenup dictates how property and spousal support will be handled if you split. It is essential if one partner is bringing significant assets (like a house or business) into the marriage, or has massive debts.
- Payoff: Saves $20,000+ in divorce litigation costs and protects pre-existing assets.
- Evidence: A (Family Law)
- Note: Both parties MUST have independent legal advice (their own lawyers) for the contract to be valid. You cannot download a template off the internet and just sign it; a judge will throw it out.

### 5. Contested divorces destroy wealth; use mediation if possible
- Cost: Mediation costs $2,000–$5,000. Litigation costs $20,000–$100,000+.
- Plain language: If you go to court to fight over custody and property, the only winners are the lawyers. A judge will eventually force a 50/50 split of the assets anyway.
- Payoff: Retains your family's wealth instead of transferring it to law firms.
- Evidence: C (Legal practice reality)
- Note: You must be separated for one year to get a divorce in Canada (unless claiming adultery or cruelty, which is rare and difficult). You can, however, sign a separation agreement immediately.

### 6. Know the legal definition of Common-Law in your province
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: The timeline for becoming "common-law" varies wildly. For the CRA (taxes), it's 12 months. For family law in Ontario (spousal support), it's 3 years (or having a child together). In BC, it's 2 years and applies to both support AND property division.
- Payoff: Prevents accidental legal obligations.
- Evidence: A (Provincial & Federal Law)
- Source: CRA; Provincial Family Law Acts.
- Note: You must declare your common-law status on your tax return. Failing to do so to get higher benefits (like the Canada Child Benefit) is tax fraud.
