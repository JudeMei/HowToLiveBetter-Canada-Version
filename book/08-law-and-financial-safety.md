# 8. Law & Financial Safety

> This chapter covers how to protect yourself from civil liability, fraud, and financial ruin.

---

### 1. Never co-sign a loan or lease unless you are willing to pay the entire amount yourself
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Co-signing does not mean you are a "reference." It means you are 100% legally responsible for the debt if the other person stops paying. The bank is asking for a co-signer because their algorithms predict the primary borrower will default.
- Payoff: Prevents financial ruin. If they default, the bank will come after you, ruin your credit, and can garnish your wages.
- Evidence: A (Contract law)
- Source: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada. Co-signing a loan.
- Note: This applies to car loans, student loans, and apartment leases for family members or partners. If you can't afford to gift them the money, don't co-sign.

### 2. Check your credit report for free once a year
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: Identity theft is common. Someone could open a credit card in your name and ruin your credit score before you apply for a mortgage. You are legally entitled to a free credit report from both Equifax and TransUnion.
- Payoff: Early detection of identity theft and errors.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada. Credit reports and scores.
- Note: You do not need to pay a monthly fee. Request the free "consumer disclosure" by mail or online. Alternatively, use free services like Borrowell or Credit Karma, but know they sell your data to market credit cards to you.

### 3. Get everything in writing
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Oral contracts are legally binding in Canada, but they are nearly impossible to prove in court. An email or text message confirming the details of an agreement saves you.
- Payoff: Protects you in Small Claims Court, LTB hearings, or employment disputes.
- Evidence: A (Civil procedure)
- Note: After a verbal agreement (with a landlord, boss, or contractor), send an email: "Just to summarize what we agreed to on the phone..." If they don't correct it, it stands as a contemporaneous record.

### 4. Understand limitation periods (The 2-year rule)
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: In most provinces (like Ontario and BC), you have exactly two years from the day you knew (or should have known) about a claim to file a lawsuit. If you wait 2 years and 1 day to sue someone for a debt or injury, the court will dismiss it.
- Payoff: Preserves your legal rights.
- Evidence: A (Limitations Act)
- Source: Limitations Act, 2002 (Ontario); equivalent provincial statutes.
- Note: Do not let a debtor string you along with promises to pay until the 2-year clock runs out. File the claim to stop the clock.

### 5. Buy Tenant Insurance if you rent
- Cost: $15–$30/month.
- Plain language: Your landlord's insurance covers the building, not your stuff. More importantly, tenant insurance provides liability coverage. If you leave the stove on and burn down the 100-unit building, the landlord's insurance company will sue YOU for millions. Tenant insurance covers this.
- Payoff: Protects against bankruptcy from accidental liability and replaces your belongings after a fire or theft.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Insurance Bureau of Canada.
- Note: It also covers "additional living expenses" (hotel costs) if your apartment becomes uninhabitable.

### 6. Never pay for a service via e-Transfer or wire transfer before it is rendered (unless it's a known, reputable business)
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Interac e-Transfers are like cash. Once the recipient deposits it, the bank will not reverse it, even if you were scammed.
- Payoff: Prevents common online frauds (Kijiji/Facebook Marketplace scams, fake rental deposits, puppy scams).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre.
- Note: Use credit cards for purchases whenever possible. Credit cards have chargeback protection; e-Transfers and debit cards do not.

### 7. Hang up on the CRA (Canada Revenue Agency) if they call you aggressively
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: The CRA will NEVER ask for payment via Bitcoin, iTunes gift cards, or Interac e-Transfer. They will NEVER threaten you with immediate arrest by the RCMP over the phone.
- Payoff: Prevents losing thousands to the most common phone scam in Canada.
- Evidence: A
- Source: CRA. Scam prevention.
- Note: If you are worried it might be real, hang up, look up the official CRA number on canada.ca, and call them yourself.

### 8. Understand common-law property division (It's not the same as marriage)
- Cost: Zero to know; a Cohabitation Agreement costs ~$1,000–$2,000.
- Plain language: In most provinces (except BC and a few others), living together for 3 years does NOT give you an automatic right to half the property when you split up. If the house is in your partner's name, you leave with nothing, unless you sue for "unjust enrichment" (expensive and hard).
- Payoff: Prevents catastrophic loss of wealth after a breakup.
- Evidence: A (Family Law)
- Source: Family Law Act (Ontario).
- Note: Provincial laws vary wildly here. BC treats common-law (2 years) almost exactly like marriage for property division. Ontario treats them completely differently. Know your province's rules. If moving in together and buying property, get a Cohabitation Agreement.
