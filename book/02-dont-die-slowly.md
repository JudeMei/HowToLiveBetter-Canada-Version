# 2. Don't Die Slowly

> The things that kill most Canadians aren't accidents — they're chronic diseases caused by smoking, drinking, sitting, eating poorly, and not sleeping enough. This chapter covers the lifestyle factors with the strongest evidence for shortening or lengthening your life, and practical ways to change them.

---

### 1. If you smoke, quit — it's the single highest-return health decision you can make
- Cost: Quitting is free. Nicotine replacement therapy (patch, gum, lozenge) is $100–$300/month but covered by most provincial drug plans and many workplace benefits. Prescription medications (varenicline/Champix, bupropion) are covered in most provinces.
- Plain language: Smoking kills half of all lifelong smokers. Quitting at any age adds years: quit at 40, gain ~9 years; quit at 50, gain ~6 years; quit at 60, gain ~4 years.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis of cohort studies (Jha P et al., 2013, NEJM) found that smokers lose on average 10+ years of life compared to never-smokers. Quitting before age 40 reduces the excess risk of death by about 90%. The British Doctors' Study (Doll R et al., 2004, BMJ, 50-year follow-up, n=34,439) confirmed that cessation at ages 60, 50, 40, and 30 gained about 3, 6, 9, and 10 years of life expectancy respectively.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Jha P et al. (2013). 21st-Century Hazards of Smoking and Benefits of Cessation in the United States. NEJM. 368:341-350. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1211128; Doll R et al. (2004). Mortality in relation to smoking: 50 years' observations on male British doctors. BMJ. 328:1519. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38142.554479.AE
- Note: Use the Canadian Cancer Society's Smokers' Helpline: 1-866-366-3667 (English), 1-866-228-5559 (French). Or text IQUIT to 123456. Provincial cessation programs (e.g., Ontario's STOP program) provide free NRT.

### 2. If you drink, stay within Canada's Low-Risk Alcohol Drinking Guidelines (or drink less)
- Cost: Saves money — alcohol is expensive. The 2023 CCSA guidance recommends no more than 2 standard drinks per week for low risk.
- Plain language: The more you drink, the higher your risk of cancer, liver disease, and death. There is no "safe" amount — but the risk is very small at 1–2 drinks per week and rises steeply beyond 6/week.
- Payoff: The Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction (2023) updated guidance based on a systematic review: at 1–2 standard drinks per week, the risk increase is minimal. At 3–6/week, risk of several cancers (breast, colon, etc.) increases. At 7+/week, risk increases significantly. A meta-analysis (GBD 2016 Alcohol Collaborators, Lancet, 2018, n=28 million) found no safe level of alcohol consumption when all health outcomes are considered, though absolute risk at low levels is small.
- Evidence: A
- Source: GBD 2016 Alcohol Collaborators. (2018). Alcohol use and burden for 195 countries and territories, 1990–2016. Lancet. 392(10152):1015-1035. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31310-2; Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction. (2023). Canada's Guidance on Alcohol and Health.
- Note: Controversy. The "no safe level" conclusion is driven primarily by cancer risk. Cardiovascular benefits of light drinking found in older studies are now thought to be largely due to methodological bias (sick-quitter effect). If you don't drink, don't start. If you drink heavily, reducing to moderate still helps significantly.

### 3. Get at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise per week (or 75 minutes vigorous)
- Cost: Free (walking, running, bodyweight exercises). A gym membership is $20–$80/month but not required.
- Plain language: Meeting the minimum exercise guideline reduces your risk of dying early by about 20–30%. Even half the recommended amount still helps substantially.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis of 9 prospective cohort studies (Arem H et al., 2015, JAMA Internal Medicine, n=661,137) found that meeting the minimum guideline of 150 min/week of moderate activity was associated with a 20% lower risk of mortality (HR 0.80) compared to inactivity. Doing 3–5× the minimum was associated with 35–39% lower mortality, with diminishing returns beyond that.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Arem H et al. (2015). Leisure Time Physical Activity and Mortality: A Detailed Pooled Analysis of the Dose-Response Relationship. JAMA Intern Med. 175(6):959-967. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.0533
- Note: Canadian Physical Activity Guidelines (CSEP) recommend 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity + muscle-strengthening 2×/week. "Moderate" = brisk walking where you can talk but not sing. Even 10 minutes of walking after meals has measurable benefits.

### 4. Break up prolonged sitting every 30–60 minutes
- Cost: Zero. Stand up, walk around for 2–3 minutes, then sit back down.
- Plain language: Sitting for 8+ hours a day with no movement breaks increases your risk of dying early by about 60% compared to sitting less than 4 hours. Standing or walking briefly every 30–60 minutes largely eliminates this excess risk.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Ekelund U et al., 2016, Lancet, n=1,005,791 from 16 studies) found that 60–75 min/day of moderate activity eliminated the increased mortality associated with 8+ hours of daily sitting. For those sitting 8+ hours and doing zero activity, the risk was ~60% higher than the least sedentary, most active group (HR ~1.60). Regular breaks in sitting reduce postprandial glucose and insulin levels.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Ekelund U et al. (2016). Does physical activity attenuate, or even eliminate, the detrimental association of sitting time with mortality? A harmonised meta-analysis of data from more than 1 million men and women. Lancet. 388(10051):1302-1310. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30370-1
- Note: This applies particularly to office workers. Set a timer or use a smartwatch reminder. A standing desk helps but isn't necessary — walking is more beneficial than standing.

### 5. Sleep 7–9 hours per night (adults 18–64)
- Cost: Zero (or minor — blackout curtains, earplugs). Requires scheduling.
- Plain language: Sleeping less than 6 hours or more than 9 hours regularly is associated with 10–30% higher mortality.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis of 16 prospective studies (Cappuccio FP et al., 2010, Sleep, n=1,382,999) found that short sleep (<6h) was associated with 12% higher mortality (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.06–1.18) and long sleep (>9h) with 30% higher mortality (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.22–1.38). The Canadian Sleep Society recommends 7–9 hours for adults.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Cappuccio FP et al. (2010). Sleep Duration and All-Cause Mortality: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Prospective Studies. Sleep. 33(5):585-592. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.5.585
- Note: Long sleep association is partially confounded by illness (reverse causation: sick people sleep more). Short sleep has stronger causal evidence from experimental studies. Prioritize consistent bed/wake times.

### 6. Eat more fruits and vegetables (aim for 7+ servings/day)
- Cost: Variable — frozen fruits and vegetables are as nutritious as fresh and often cheaper ($1–$3/bag). Canada's Food Guide recommends half your plate be fruits and vegetables.
- Plain language: Eating 7+ servings of fruits and vegetables per day is associated with about 25–30% lower risk of dying early.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis of 16 prospective cohort studies (Wang X et al., 2014, BMJ, n=833,234) found that each additional serving of fruits and vegetables per day was associated with a 5% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR 0.95 per serving), up to about 5 servings/day for all-cause mortality. A separate analysis (Aune D et al., 2017, Int J Epidemiol) found benefits up to 10 servings/day (800g), with a 24% reduction in heart disease, 33% in stroke, and 31% in premature death.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Wang X et al. (2014). Fruit and vegetable consumption and mortality from all causes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies. BMJ. 349:g4490. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4490; Aune D et al. (2017). Fruit and vegetable intake and the risk of cardiovascular disease, total cancer and all-cause mortality. Int J Epidemiol. 46(3):1029-1056. https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw319
- Note: Frozen, canned (low sodium), and fresh all count. Juice does not count the same way (lacks fiber, concentrates sugar). Canada's Food Guide moved away from portion counting — "fill half your plate" is the new guidance.

### 7. Reduce consumption of processed and ultra-processed foods
- Cost: Saves money long-term (cooking from scratch is cheaper than packaged food in most cases).
- Plain language: A 10% increase in ultra-processed food consumption is associated with a 14% increase in all-cause mortality.
- Payoff: A prospective cohort study (Rico-Campà A et al., 2019, BMJ, n=19,899, 10-year follow-up) found that each additional 10% of energy from ultra-processed food was associated with a 14% increase in all-cause mortality (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.01–1.29). A larger meta-analysis (Lane MM et al., 2024, BMJ, 45 pooled analyses, n=9.9 million) confirmed associations with cardiovascular mortality, type 2 diabetes, depression, and cancer.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Rico-Campà A et al. (2019). Association between consumption of ultra-processed foods and all cause mortality: SUN prospective cohort study. BMJ. 365:l1949. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l1949; Lane MM et al. (2024). Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes. BMJ. 384:e077310. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310
- Note: Ultra-processed (NOVA Group 4) includes soft drinks, packaged snacks, instant noodles, chicken nuggets, many breakfast cereals. Look for short ingredient lists with recognizable foods. Canada's Food Guide emphasizes cooking more and eating less processed food.

### 8. Limit processed meat (bacon, sausage, deli meats, hot dogs)
- Cost: Saves money — processed meat is expensive per protein gram compared to legumes, eggs, or chicken.
- Plain language: Eating 50g of processed meat daily (about 2 slices of bacon) increases colorectal cancer risk by 18%.
- Payoff: The IARC (WHO) classified processed meat as a Group 1 carcinogen in 2015. A meta-analysis (Chan DSM et al., 2011, PLoS One) found that each 50g/day increase in processed meat consumption was associated with an 18% increase in colorectal cancer risk (RR 1.18, 95% CI 1.10–1.28).
- Evidence: A
- Source: IARC. (2015). Red Meat and Processed Meat. IARC Monographs Vol. 114. https://publications.iarc.fr/Book-And-Report-Series/Iarc-Monographs-On-The-Identification-Of-Carcinogenic-Hazards-To-Humans/Red-Meat-And-Processed-Meat-2018; Chan DSM et al. (2011). Red and processed meat and colorectal cancer incidence: meta-analysis of prospective studies. PLoS One. 6(6):e20456. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0020456
- Note: "Processed meat" means meat preserved by smoking, curing, salting, or adding preservatives (nitrites). Unprocessed red meat has weaker evidence of harm.

### 9. If you vape and never smoked, stop — vaping is not risk-free
- Cost: Saves $100–$300/month.
- Plain language: Vaping is less harmful than smoking, but it's not harmless. It delivers nicotine (addictive), ultrafine particles, and volatile organic compounds. Long-term health effects are unknown because vaping hasn't existed long enough for long-term studies.
- Payoff: Health Canada and the Canadian Thoracic Society advise that vaping is significantly less harmful than smoking for adult smokers switching, but non-smokers and youth should not start. Vaping is associated with increased respiratory symptoms and nicotine addiction. Cases of e-cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury (EVALI) were linked primarily to illicit THC cartridges containing vitamin E acetate.
- Evidence: B
- Source: National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. (2018). Public Health Consequences of E-Cigarettes. https://doi.org/10.17226/24952; Health Canada. Vaping. https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/smoking-tobacco/vaping.html
- Note: If you currently smoke, switching to vaping is a net health gain. If you've never smoked, don't vape. The goal is to be free of both. Youth vaping rates in Canada have risen sharply — talk to your kids.

### 10. Limit added sugar intake to less than 10% of daily calories
- Cost: Saves money (sugary drinks and snacks are expensive).
- Plain language: Drinking 1–2 sugary drinks per day increases type 2 diabetes risk by about 26% and weight gain.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Imamura F et al., 2015, BMJ) found that habitual consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages was associated with a 26% greater incidence of type 2 diabetes per serving per day (RR 1.26, 95% CI 1.12–1.41) and 12% greater incidence of cardiovascular disease. WHO recommends <10% of total energy from free sugars; conditional recommendation for <5%.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Imamura F et al. (2015). Consumption of sugar sweetened beverages, artificially sweetened beverages, and fruit juice and incidence of type 2 diabetes: systematic review, meta-analysis, and estimation of population attributable fraction. BMJ. 351:h3576. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.h3576
- Note: One can of soda has ~40g of sugar (10 teaspoons). Fruit juice has comparable sugar content — eat the fruit instead. Canada's Food Guide recommends making water your drink of choice.

### 11. Switch to using unsaturated cooking oils (olive oil, canola oil) instead of butter or lard for everyday cooking
- Cost: Neutral to slightly cheaper per use. Canola oil (a Canadian invention) is widely available and affordable.
- Plain language: Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated fats reduces cardiovascular death by about 20–30%.
- Payoff: A Cochrane review (Hooper L et al., 2020, 15 RCTs, n=59,000+) found that reducing saturated fat and replacing it with unsaturated fat reduced cardiovascular events by 17% (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.72–0.96). Benefits were clearest when replacement was with polyunsaturated fats. A pooled analysis of Harvard cohorts (Li Y et al., 2015, JAMA Intern Med) found replacing 5% of calories from saturated fat with equivalent calories from polyunsaturated fat was associated with 25% lower coronary heart disease risk (HR 0.75).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Hooper L et al. (2020). Reduction in saturated fat intake for cardiovascular disease. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. (5):CD011737. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD011737.pub3; Li Y et al. (2015). Saturated Fats Compared With Unsaturated Fats and Sources of Carbohydrates in Relation to Risk of Coronary Heart Disease. JAMA Intern Med. 175(9):1454-1463. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.2993
- Note: Canola oil has the lowest saturated fat of common oils and is high in omega-3s. Extra-virgin olive oil has the most evidence for overall health benefit. Avoid partially hydrogenated oils (trans fats) — Health Canada banned them in 2018.

### 12. Don't use cannabis heavily or start before age 25
- Cost: Saves money ($200–$500/month for daily users).
- Plain language: Regular cannabis use before age 25 is associated with lasting effects on brain development, lower IQ, and increased risk of psychosis. Heavy adult use is associated with chronic bronchitis and cannabis use disorder.
- Payoff: A longitudinal study (Meier MH et al., 2012, PNAS, n=1,037, Dunedin cohort, 25-year follow-up) found that persistent cannabis use starting in adolescence was associated with a decline of up to 8 IQ points by age 38 that was not fully restored after quitting. A meta-analysis (Marconi A et al., 2016, Schizophr Bull) found heavy cannabis use was associated with a ~3× higher risk of psychotic disorders (OR 3.90, 95% CI 2.84–5.34).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Meier MH et al. (2012). Persistent cannabis users show neuropsychological decline from childhood to midlife. PNAS. 109(40):E2657-E2664. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1206820109; Marconi A et al. (2016). Meta-analysis of the association between the level of cannabis use and risk of psychosis. Schizophr Bull. 42(5):1262-1269. https://doi.org/10.1093/schbul/sbw003
- Note: Cannabis is legal in Canada (Cannabis Act, 2018) for adults 18+ or 19+ (varies by province). Legal doesn't mean safe. The Canada's Lower-Risk Cannabis Use Guidelines recommend: don't start before 25, avoid synthetic cannabis, don't smoke it (use edibles/vaporizers instead), avoid daily/near-daily use.

### 13. Get help for alcohol use disorder — it's a medical condition, not a moral failing
- Cost: Free through provincial addiction services. Detox programs, counselling, and medications (naltrexone, acamprosate) are covered in most provinces.
- Plain language: Alcohol use disorder affects ~6% of Canadians. Medical treatment (medication + counselling) roughly doubles the chance of sustained abstinence or reduced drinking compared to willpower alone.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Jonas DE et al., 2014, JAMA) found that naltrexone reduced return to heavy drinking by about 17% compared to placebo (NNT = 12), and acamprosate increased continuous abstinence (NNT = 12). Combined pharmacotherapy + behavioral intervention showed the strongest results. Untreated AUD reduces life expectancy by 24–28 years (Westman J et al., 2015, Addiction).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Jonas DE et al. (2014). Pharmacotherapy for Adults With Alcohol Use Disorders in Outpatient Settings: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. JAMA. 311(18):1889-1900. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2014.3628; Westman J et al. (2015). Mortality and life expectancy of people with alcohol use disorder in Denmark, Finland and Sweden. Acta Psychiatr Scand. 131(4):297-306. https://doi.org/10.1111/acps.12330
- Note: ConnexOntario: 1-866-531-2600. BC Alcohol & Drug Information: 1-800-663-1441. Alberta Addiction Helpline: 1-866-332-2322. Most provinces have centralized intake lines. Detoxification from heavy alcohol use can be life-threatening — do not quit cold turkey without medical supervision.

### 14. Eat a handful of nuts (30g/day) — especially tree nuts and peanuts
- Cost: $0.30–$0.50/day. Unsalted is best.
- Plain language: Eating a handful of nuts daily is associated with about 20% lower risk of dying from any cause.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis of 20 prospective cohort studies (Aune D et al., 2016, BMC Medicine, n=819,448) found that 28g/day of nut consumption was associated with a 22% reduction in all-cause mortality (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.72–0.84), 21% reduction in cardiovascular disease, 15% reduction in cancer mortality.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Aune D et al. (2016). Nut consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease, total cancer, all-cause and cause-specific mortality: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. BMC Medicine. 14:207. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-016-0730-3
- Note: Peanuts (technically a legume) show similar benefit to tree nuts. Unsalted, raw or dry-roasted preferred. Allergies are real — this item is for people who can safely eat nuts.

### 15. Eat whole grains instead of refined grains (brown rice, whole wheat, oats)
- Cost: Minimal — whole wheat bread and oats are comparably priced to white bread.
- Plain language: Eating 3 servings of whole grains per day is associated with about 17% lower risk of dying.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Aune D et al., 2016, BMJ, n=786,076 from 45 studies) found that 90g/day of whole grains (about 3 servings) was associated with a 17% reduction in all-cause mortality (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.77–0.90), 22% reduction in cardiovascular disease mortality, and 15% reduction in cancer mortality.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Aune D et al. (2016). Whole grain consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all cause and cause specific mortality: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. BMJ. 353:i2716. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i2716
- Note: "Whole grain" = the grain includes the bran, germ, and endosperm. Check ingredient lists — "multigrain" or "wheat bread" doesn't mean whole grain. Oatmeal, brown rice, quinoa, and true whole-wheat products count.

### 16. If you use nicotine replacement to quit smoking, use combination therapy (patch + gum/lozenge)
- Cost: $150–$300/month; covered by most provincial drug plans and many employer benefits. Free through some provincial quit-smoking programs.
- Plain language: Using the nicotine patch AND a fast-acting form (gum or lozenge) together roughly doubles your chance of quitting compared to using nothing.
- Payoff: A Cochrane review (Lindson N et al., 2019) found that combining a nicotine patch with a faster-acting form of NRT increased quit rates by 15–36% compared to a single form of NRT (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.15–1.36). Varenicline (Champix) is the most effective single medication (RR 2.24 vs. placebo).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Lindson N et al. (2019). Different doses, durations and modes of delivery of nicotine replacement therapy for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013308; Cahill K et al. (2013). Nicotine receptor partial agonists for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006103.pub7
- Note: Talk to your pharmacist — in many provinces, pharmacists can prescribe NRT directly without a doctor visit. Smokers' Helpline: 1-866-366-3667.

### 17. Use turn on your kitchen range hood when cooking with gas or oil
- Cost: Zero (you already have one in most kitchens).
- Plain language: Cooking with gas produces nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) at levels that exceed outdoor air quality standards. Using the range hood reduces indoor NO₂ by 50–80%.
- Payoff: A study (Paulin LM et al., 2014, AJRCCM) found that gas cooking without ventilation produces indoor NO₂ levels exceeding Health Canada's residential indoor air quality guideline (90 ppb). Range hood use reduced personal NO₂ exposure by 50–80% depending on hood type and use pattern. Chronic NO₂ exposure is associated with increased respiratory symptoms and reduced lung function, particularly in children.
- Evidence: B
- Source: Paulin LM et al. (2014). Home interventions are effective at decreasing indoor nitrogen dioxide concentrations. Indoor Air. 24(4):416-24. https://doi.org/10.1111/ina.12085; Health Canada. Residential Indoor Air Quality Guidelines: Nitrogen Dioxide.
- Note: Also applies to any high-heat oil cooking (stir-frying, deep-frying) — cooking oil fumes contain polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Vent-to-outdoor hoods are far more effective than recirculating hoods.

### 18. Don't use tanning beds
- Cost: Saves $50–$100/session.
- Plain language: Tanning bed use before age 35 increases melanoma risk by 75%. Melanoma is the deadliest form of skin cancer.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Boniol M et al., 2012, BMJ, 27 studies) found that ever-use of indoor tanning was associated with a 20% increase in melanoma risk (RR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08–1.34). First use before age 35 increased risk by 75% (RR 1.75, 95% CI 1.35–2.26). The IARC classifies UV-emitting tanning devices as Group 1 carcinogens.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Boniol M et al. (2012). Cutaneous melanoma attributable to sunbed use: systematic review and meta-analysis. BMJ. 345:e4757. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.e4757; IARC Monographs Vol. 100D.
- Note: Tanning beds are banned for minors in several Canadian provinces (Ontario, BC, Quebec, Manitoba, PEI, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick). Some provinces have proposed full bans. There is no such thing as a "safe" indoor tan.

### 19. Limit alcohol-free days — don't binge drink on weekends even if your weekly total is "moderate"
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Drinking 7 drinks in one sitting on Saturday is far worse than 1 drink each day of the week, even though the weekly total is the same. Binge drinking (4+ drinks women, 5+ drinks men in ~2 hours) increases stroke and heart attack risk acutely.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Roerecke M, Rehm J, 2014, BMC Medicine) found that heavy episodic drinking (binge pattern) was associated with significantly higher risk of ischemic heart disease mortality (RR 1.45, 95% CI 1.24–1.70) compared to the same weekly volume spread evenly. Binge drinking is associated with acute alcohol poisoning (cause of ~300 deaths/year in Canada).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Roerecke M, Rehm J. (2014). Alcohol consumption, drinking patterns, and ischemic heart disease: a narrative review of meta-analyses and a systematic review and meta-analysis of the impact of heavy drinking occasions on risk for moderate drinkers. BMC Medicine. 12:182. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-014-0182-6
- Note: Canada's updated alcohol guidance (2023) specifically warns against binge drinking. The definition of "binge" is lower than most people think: 4 drinks in 2 hours for women, 5 for men.

### 20. Get a blood pressure check at least once every 2 years (annually if over 40)
- Cost: Free at any pharmacy (most have automated machines) or at your doctor. Home monitors: $50–$100.
- Plain language: High blood pressure has no symptoms until it causes a stroke or heart attack. 1 in 4 Canadian adults has hypertension; half of them don't know it. Treatment reduces stroke risk by 35–40%.
- Payoff: Hypertension Canada estimates that ~7.5 million Canadians have hypertension, and about 17% are unaware. A meta-analysis (Ettehad D et al., 2016, Lancet, 123 trials, n=613,815) found that each 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure reduced stroke by 27% (RR 0.73), heart failure by 28%, coronary heart disease by 17%, and all-cause mortality by 13% (RR 0.87).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Ettehad D et al. (2016). Blood pressure lowering for prevention of cardiovascular disease and death: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Lancet. 387(10022):957-967. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)01225-8; Hypertension Canada. 2020 Guidelines.
- Note: Normal: <120/80. Elevated: 120–129/<80. High: 130+/80+. If you're told your blood pressure is high, follow up with your doctor — medication is cheap, well-tolerated, and dramatically reduces risk.

### 21. Eat less sodium — aim for under 2,300 mg/day
- Cost: Free (just use less salt and check labels).
- Plain language: The average Canadian eats about 3,400 mg of sodium per day — well above the recommended limit. Most of it comes from processed and restaurant food, not your salt shaker.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Aburto NJ et al., 2013, BMJ, 36 studies) found that reduced sodium intake was associated with a 24% reduction in stroke risk (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.59–0.98) and a reduction in blood pressure (mean reduction: -3.39 mmHg systolic). Health Canada recommends adults limit sodium to 2,300 mg/day.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Aburto NJ et al. (2013). Effect of lower sodium intake on health: systematic review and meta-analyses. BMJ. 346:f1326. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.f1326; Health Canada. Sodium in Canada. https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/sodium.html
- Note: Top sodium sources in the Canadian diet: bread, processed meat, cheese, soups, sauces, and restaurant/takeout food. Cooking at home using fresh ingredients is the most effective way to reduce sodium intake.

### 22. Aim for naps under 30 minutes — avoid long daytime naps
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: Short naps (under 30 minutes) improve alertness. Long naps (60+ minutes) are associated with a 30% higher risk of mortality — likely because they disrupt nighttime sleep and may indicate underlying illness.
- Payoff: A meta-analysis (Zhong G et al., 2016, Sleep Medicine, 11 studies, n=190,946) found that napping >60 minutes was associated with a 30% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.09–1.54). Napping <30 minutes was not associated with increased risk and may have been protective for cardiovascular events.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Zhong G et al. (2016). Daytime Napping and Mortality from All Causes, Cardiovascular Disease, and Cancer: A Meta-analysis of Prospective Cohort Studies. Sleep Medicine. 21:100-108. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2016.01.003
- Note: Reverse causation likely explains some of this — sick people nap more. But if you're healthy and napping regularly for 60+ minutes, it may be a sign of inadequate nighttime sleep. Fix the underlying sleep issue.
