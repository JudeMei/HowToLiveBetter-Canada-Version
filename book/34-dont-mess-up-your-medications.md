# 34. Don't Mess Up Your Medications

> Improper use of over-the-counter and prescription medications causes thousands of preventable hospitalizations in Canada each year. This chapter covers the most common and dangerous errors.

---

### 1. Do not exceed the maximum daily dose of Tylenol (Acetaminophen)
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Tylenol is safe at normal doses, but it has a very narrow window of safety. The maximum daily dose for an adult is 4,000 mg (often lower if you drink alcohol or have liver issues). Taking 7,000 mg in one day can cause acute, irreversible liver failure.
- Payoff: Prevents needing a liver transplant or dying.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Health Canada. Acetaminophen safety.
- Note: Acetaminophen is hidden in hundreds of cold and flu medications (e.g., NyQuil, NeoCitran). People accidentally overdose by taking Tylenol for a headache AND NyQuil for a cold, without realizing they are doubling up on the same drug. Always read the label.

### 2. Do not mix alcohol with sedatives or painkillers
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Mixing alcohol with benzodiazepines (Ativan, Xanax), sleeping pills (Zopiclone), or opioids (Percocet, OxyContin) severely depresses your central nervous system.
- Payoff: Prevents you from simply stopping breathing in your sleep.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction.
- Note: The synergistic effect of these drugs means that 1 + 1 does not equal 2; it equals 5. The combination is a primary cause of accidental overdose deaths.

### 3. Finish your entire course of antibiotics (unless directed otherwise)
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: If your doctor prescribes 7 days of antibiotics for an infection, do not stop taking them on day 4 just because you feel better.
- Payoff: Prevents the infection from returning stronger and contributing to global antibiotic resistance (superbugs).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Public Health Agency of Canada. Antimicrobial resistance.
- Note: If you have leftover antibiotics, do not save them for next time, and do not flush them down the toilet. Return them to a pharmacy for safe disposal.

### 4. Check for drug interactions (Grapefruit Juice is a real issue)
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Grapefruit juice blocks an enzyme in your liver (CYP3A4) that breaks down many common medications, including statins (for cholesterol) and some blood pressure drugs. Drinking grapefruit juice can cause the drug levels in your blood to spike to toxic levels.
- Payoff: Prevents accidental toxicity and muscle damage.
- Evidence: A
- Source: CMAJ. Grapefruit-medication interactions.
- Note: Always use a single pharmacy for all your prescriptions so the pharmacist's computer system can automatically check for interactions between your different medications.

### 5. Carry a list of your medications (and doses) in your wallet
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: If you are brought to the ER unconscious, the doctors need to know what you are taking. "A little white pill for my heart" is not helpful.
- Payoff: Prevents lethal drug interactions administered in the ER.
- Evidence: B
- Note: Include any over-the-counter supplements you take daily (like St. John's Wort or iron), as these can interact powerfully with hospital medications.

### 6. Know the difference between Ibuprofen and Acetaminophen
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: 
  - **Acetaminophen (Tylenol)**: Good for pain and fever. Processed by the liver. Does NOT reduce inflammation.
  - **Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin)**: Good for pain, fever, AND inflammation. Processed by the kidneys. Can irritate the stomach lining and increase bleeding risk.
- Payoff: Taking the right drug for the right problem safely.
- Evidence: A
- Note: You can usually alternate between the two (e.g., taking Tylenol, then 3 hours later taking Advil) for severe pain or a stubborn fever, but ask your pharmacist first.
