# 7. How to Survive When You're Broke

> If you lose your job, face a sudden eviction, or run out of money, pride won't feed you. This chapter covers the Canadian safety net: what you are legally entitled to, and where to get immediate help.

---

### 1. Apply for Employment Insurance (EI) immediately after losing your job
- Cost: Zero. Apply online via Service Canada.
- Plain language: If you lost your job through no fault of your own (layoff, shortage of work), you are entitled to up to 55% of your earnings (max $668/week in 2024) for 14 to 45 weeks. Apply the day you stop working — waiting can cause you to lose benefits.
- Payoff: Prevents immediate financial collapse. EI is an insurance program you paid into via payroll deductions; it is not welfare.
- Evidence: A (Federal policy)
- Source: Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC). Employment Insurance benefits.
- Note: Even if you haven't received your Record of Employment (ROE) from your employer yet, apply immediately. Service Canada will chase the employer for the ROE. Do not delay.

### 2. If you don't qualify for EI and have no money, apply for Provincial Social Assistance
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Every province has a "welfare" or income assistance program (e.g., Ontario Works, BC Income Assistance, Alberta Income Support). It pays very little (often under $1,000/month), but it provides emergency cash for food and shelter.
- Payoff: Basic survival. It also opens the door to other benefits (prescription drug coverage, emergency dental, transit passes).
- Evidence: A (Provincial policy)
- Source: Provincial ministry websites (e.g., Ontario Ministry of Children, Community and Social Services).
- Note: You must exhaust other options (like EI or savings) first. The application process is intrusive (they check your bank statements).

### 3. Use food banks without shame
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Food banks exist for emergencies. If you have to choose between paying rent and buying food, pay rent and go to the food bank.
- Payoff: Frees up scarce cash to prevent eviction or utility shut-off.
- Evidence: C
- Source: Food Banks Canada.
- Note: Call 211 (available across Canada) to find the nearest food bank and their requirements (most require ID and proof of address/income).

### 4. Apply for utility relief programs before your power is cut
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Provinces have programs to stop utility shut-offs for low-income households (e.g., LEAP or OESP in Ontario). They will pay your arrears directly to the utility company.
- Payoff: Keeps the lights and heat on during winter. (Note: It is illegal for utility companies in most provinces to disconnect power during the winter months, typically Nov 15 to April 30).
- Evidence: A (Provincial policy)
- Source: Ontario Energy Board; equivalent regulators in other provinces.
- Note: Do not ignore utility bills. Call them, say you are in financial hardship, and ask for a payment plan and information on relief programs.

### 5. If your landlord serves an eviction notice for unpaid rent, do not move out immediately
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: A notice from a landlord (e.g., an N4 in Ontario) is NOT an eviction order. Only the provincial tenancy board (e.g., LTB) can legally order an eviction, and only a sheriff/bailiff can enforce it.
- Payoff: Buys you time (often several months) to find the money, negotiate a payment plan, or find a new place. Moving out immediately guarantees you are homeless.
- Evidence: A (Provincial law)
- Source: Residential Tenancies Act (Ontario); equivalent provincial acts.
- Note: Seek free legal advice from a community legal clinic immediately. Rent banks (municipal programs) offer zero-interest loans or grants to pay rent arrears and stop evictions.

### 6. Do not go to payday lenders
- Cost: Prevents a debt death-spiral.
- Plain language: Payday loans (e.g., Money Mart) charge the maximum legal interest rate (equivalent to ~390% annually). If you borrow $300 to pay rent, you will owe the principal plus massive fees out of your next cheque, leaving you broke again.
- Payoff: Avoids catastrophic debt traps.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada. Payday loans.
- Note: If you are truly out of options, use a food bank or rent bank. A payday loan only delays the crisis by two weeks and makes it twice as expensive.

### 7. File your taxes even if you had zero income
- Cost: Free (use Wealthsimple Tax or a volunteer tax clinic).
- Plain language: The government owes you money (GST/HST credit, Canada Child Benefit, Canada Workers Benefit, Carbon Tax Rebate). You ONLY get this money if you file a tax return.
- Payoff: Hundreds or thousands of dollars in tax-free government cheques.
- Evidence: A (Federal policy)
- Source: Canada Revenue Agency (CRA).
- Note: Low-income individuals can use the Community Volunteer Income Tax Program (CVITP) to get their taxes done for free.

### 8. If you can't afford your prescriptions, apply for provincial drug coverage
- Cost: Zero to apply.
- Plain language: If you are low-income and not on social assistance, provinces have programs to cover catastrophic drug costs (e.g., Trillium Drug Program in Ontario, Fair PharmaCare in BC).
- Payoff: Covers the cost of life-saving medications. Deductibles are based on a percentage of your income (e.g., 4% in Ontario).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Provincial Ministry of Health websites.
- Note: Do not ration essential medications (like insulin). Speak to a pharmacist — they can often help you navigate the paperwork for provincial coverage or compassionate care programs from drug manufacturers.
