# 29. After a Major Blow

> When your world falls apart—due to a death, a severe diagnosis, or a sudden job loss—the psychological and physical toll is immense. This chapter covers how to survive the immediate aftermath.

---

### 1. Beware the cardiovascular risk after losing a loved one
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: "Broken heart syndrome" is real. The stress of acute grief significantly increases your risk of a heart attack or stroke in the first 30 days after a spouse or close loved one dies.
- Payoff: Awareness can save your life. If you feel chest pain or shortness of breath while grieving, do not dismiss it as "just stress." Go to the ER.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Carey IM et al. (2014). Increased risk of acute cardiovascular events after partner bereavement: a matched cohort study. JAMA Intern Med. 174(4):598-605.
- Note: Make sure you continue taking your prescribed medications (like blood pressure pills) during this chaotic time.

### 2. Delay major life decisions for at least 6 to 12 months
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: When in extreme grief or shock (e.g., a terminal diagnosis, sudden widowhood), your brain's executive function is compromised. Do not sell your house, quit your job, or move to a new city while in acute shock.
- Payoff: Prevents catastrophic financial and life errors made in a state of panic.
- Evidence: C (Psychological consensus)
- Note: Keep everything as stable as possible until the initial fog lifts.

### 3. Seek immediate help if you have a plan for suicide
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: If the pain is unbearable and you are making a specific plan to end your life, you are in a medical emergency. The brain is lying to you about the permanence of the situation. Call or text 9-8-8 immediately.
- Payoff: Keeps you alive until the acute crisis passes.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Public Health Agency of Canada. 9-8-8: Suicide Crisis Helpline.
- Note: 9-8-8 is a free, confidential service available 24/7 across Canada. You can text or call.

### 4. For a terminal or severe diagnosis: Record your appointments
- Cost: Free.
- Plain language: When a doctor says the word "cancer" or "ALS," you will go deaf to everything else they say for the next 20 minutes. Bring a trusted friend to take notes, or ask the doctor if you can record the conversation on your phone.
- Payoff: Ensures you don't miss critical treatment instructions or timelines.
- Evidence: B
- Note: Always ask the doctor's permission before recording. Most are highly empathetic to this request.

### 5. Access grief counselling or support groups
- Cost: Free (through community organizations or hospices) or covered by EAP/benefits.
- Plain language: Grief is not a disease, but "complicated grief" (where you are entirely incapacitated for over a year) requires professional help.
- Payoff: Helps you process the loss and eventually integrate it into your life.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Shear MK et al. (2014). Treatment of complicated grief in elderly persons: a randomized clinical trial. JAMA Psychiatry. 71(11):1287-1295.
- Note: Look for local hospices; they often offer free bereavement support groups even if your loved one didn't die there.

### 6. Do not isolate yourself entirely
- Cost: Emotional effort.
- Plain language: The instinct after a major blow is to hide from the world. While you need rest, total isolation removes your safety net and increases the risk of severe depression.
- Payoff: Prevents a downward spiral into clinical depression.
- Evidence: A
- Note: Allow a few trusted people to bring you food or sit with you in silence. You do not have to "entertain" them.
