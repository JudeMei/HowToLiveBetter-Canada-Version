# 19. Employment, Termination & Workplace Injury

> Your employer has legal obligations to you, even when they fire you. This chapter covers your rights under Canadian employment law.

---

### 1. Never sign a severance offer immediately upon being fired
- Cost: ~$300 for an employment lawyer consultation.
- Plain language: When you are fired (without cause), the minimum severance required by the provincial Employment Standards Act (ESA) is often vastly lower than what you are entitled to under "common law." HR will hand you a letter and pressure you to sign it immediately to get a slightly better deal. Don't. You have the right to have a lawyer review it.
- Payoff: Common law severance can be up to 24 months of pay (depending on age, tenure, and position), whereas ESA minimums might only be 8 weeks.
- Evidence: A (Employment Law)
- Source: Canadian Common Law (Bardal factors).
- Note: If you are fired "with cause" (e.g., theft, gross insubordination), you get nothing, but true "cause" is very difficult for an employer to prove in Canada.

### 2. Document everything if you are being pushed out (Constructive Dismissal)
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: If your employer cuts your pay, demotes you, or makes your work environment incredibly toxic to force you to quit, it's called "constructive dismissal." The law treats it as if you were fired, meaning you are owed severance.
- Payoff: Secures your right to severance pay.
- Evidence: A (Employment Law)
- Note: Forward relevant emails to your personal account (do not forward confidential company IP, just HR correspondence regarding your employment conditions). Do not resign before speaking to an employment lawyer.

### 3. Report workplace injuries to the Workers' Compensation Board immediately
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: If you get hurt at work (even a repetitive strain injury like carpal tunnel), report it to your employer immediately AND file a claim with your provincial workers' compensation board (e.g., WSIB in Ontario, WorkSafeBC).
- Payoff: Ensures your medical expenses are covered and you receive replacement income while you recover.
- Evidence: A (Provincial Law)
- Source: Provincial Workers' Compensation Acts.
- Note: Do not let your boss talk you out of filing a claim by offering to "pay you under the table while you rest." If the injury becomes permanent, you will have no official record and no long-term support.

### 4. Know your right to refuse unsafe work
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: Under Occupational Health and Safety laws across Canada, you have the legal right to refuse work that you believe is unsafe to yourself or others. Your employer cannot legally fire or discipline you for exercising this right.
- Payoff: Prevents serious injury or death.
- Evidence: A (Provincial/Federal OHS Law)
- Source: Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (CCOHS).
- Note: There is a specific procedure you must follow (report to supervisor, joint health and safety committee, then ministry inspector). You must remain at work in a safe place during the investigation.

### 5. Check your pay stub for Vacation Pay
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: By law, you are entitled to at least 2 weeks of paid vacation per year (3 weeks after 5 years, in most provinces), or 4% vacation pay added to your cheques if you are an hourly/part-time worker.
- Payoff: Ensures you are not being underpaid.
- Evidence: A (Employment Standards Act)
- Source: Provincial Employment Standards.
- Note: Unpaid wages and vacation pay can be claimed by filing a complaint with the provincial Ministry of Labour, which is free and does not require a lawyer.

### 6. Know if you are federally or provincially regulated
- Cost: Zero.
- Plain language: 90% of Canadian workers are covered by their provincial Employment Standards Act. About 10% (banking, telecommunications, interprovincial transport, federal government) are covered by the federal Canada Labour Code.
- Payoff: Ensures you are looking at the right rulebook for overtime, minimum wage, and termination rights.
- Evidence: A
- Source: Government of Canada. Federally regulated businesses and industries.
