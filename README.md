# 🍁 How to Live Better — Canada Edition

**An evidence-based guide to living longer, staying healthier, saving money, and staying out of trouble in Canada.**

How to die less, get sick less, and what to do in emergencies. How to stop wasting money and avoid scams, lawsuits, and criminal charges. What to claim when you're unemployed or broke. How to start a business, rent or buy a home, navigate relationships, raise kids, and build skills. Legal, healthcare, tax, and benefits content follows current Canadian federal and provincial rules.

600+ recommendations. Each one states the cost, the payoff, the strength of evidence, and the original source — only peer-reviewed journals, government publications, and official statistics. No influencer advice, no hearsay.

**You don't have to do everything.** This is a menu sorted by value-for-effort, not a to-do list. Pick one or two things and you're already ahead. The author hasn't done most of these either.

## How to Read Each Item

Each recommendation looks like this:

```markdown
### 5. Switch your table salt to a low-sodium salt (potassium salt)
- Cost: A bag costs a couple dollars more. You switch it next time you buy salt — no extra time. Taste barely changes.
- Plain language: For people who've had a stroke or are over 60 with high blood pressure, switching to low-sodium salt lowered death by about 12% and stroke by about 14% over five years.
- Payoff: An RCT with 20,995 people in rural China (stroke survivors or hypertensive adults 60+), followed for 4.74 years. The low-sodium salt group had 12% lower mortality (RR 0.88), 14% fewer strokes (RR 0.86), and 13% fewer major cardiovascular events (RR 0.87).
- Evidence: A
- Source: Neal B et al. (2021). Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death. NEJM. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675
- Note: Controversy. The trial subjects were high-risk elderly; healthy young people would see a much smaller benefit. People with kidney problems or on potassium-sparing medications should consult a doctor first.
```

## Four Resources This Guide Helps You Keep

- **Lifespan**: Live longer, die less from preventable causes
- **Time & Energy**: Don't burn your waking hours on zero-return activities
- **Money**: Stop wasting money; spend where the return is proven
- **Freedom**: Don't lose your liberty because you didn't know about a legal boundary

Each item answers two questions: *What does it cost?* (money / time / energy / willpower) and *What do you get back?* (lower mortality / money saved / time saved / legal protection).

Items are sorted by value-for-effort within each chapter, not by category: the ones that cost almost nothing and return the most go first.

## Evidence Grading

Every item is tagged with an evidence grade:

| Grade | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| A | Has specific numbers from meta-analyses, large cohort studies, or RCTs — can state how much the risk dropped (HR, RR, percentage). For legal/policy items: cites the actual statute or regulation. |
| B | Has research support but no precise number; or only one small study supports it |
| C | Author's experience, or widely accepted practice with no direct research |

Of 634 items: A-grade 410, B-grade 172, C-grade 52. Items with controversy are flagged. All sources cite original literature (journal articles with DOI/PubMed links, or government publications from Statistics Canada, Health Canada, CRA, ESDC, IRCC, etc.). No secondhand summaries.

## Value-for-Effort Tiers

| Dimension | Values | How it's determined |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Category | Lifespan / Money / Time-Energy / Freedom | What the item primarily gets you back |
| Payoff size | Large / Medium / Small | Lifespan: ≥20% reduction = large, 10–20% = medium, <10% = small. Money: $10K+ = large, $500–$10K = medium, <$500 = small. Freedom: avoiding criminal charges = large, avoiding fines = medium, avoiding civil disputes = small |
| Value-for-effort | Exceptional / High / Moderate | Large payoff + zero cost = exceptional. Large payoff + low cost, or medium payoff + zero cost = high. Everything else = moderate |

**This tier is the author's judgment, not evidence** — by this guide's own standards, it's C-grade. "Moderate" doesn't mean "don't do it" — every item in this guide is recommended; it just means you should weigh the cost yourself.

<details>
<summary>Expand glossary: 35 terms (all-cause mortality, HR, RR, 95% CI, meta-analysis, BMI, RRSP, TFSA, CPP, EI…)</summary>

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| All-cause mortality | The proportion of people in a group who died during a period, regardless of cause. This guide uses it to measure "how long you live." |
| HR | Hazard ratio. How fast the intervention group experienced events (death, disease) compared to the control group. HR 0.87 = 13% lower than the control. |
| RR | Relative risk. The ratio of event probability between two groups. Read the same way as HR. |
| OR | Odds ratio. Also compares two groups, but uses a slightly different calculation. When the event is rare, it's close to RR; when the event is common, it exaggerates the difference. |
| 95% CI | 95% confidence interval. Research numbers always have uncertainty — this is the range where the true value very likely falls. If the range crosses 1.0 (for ratios) or 0 (for differences), the difference may just be chance. |
| RCT | Randomized controlled trial. People are randomly split into two groups — one does the thing, one doesn't. Best way to show causation. |
| Meta-analysis | Combines results from many studies to produce a single overall number. |
| Cohort study | Follows a large group for years and records who has what outcomes. Shows correlation, not necessarily causation. |
| Observational study | Researchers only observe and record — they don't assign who does what. Results can be skewed by confounders. |
| Confounding | A third factor influences both the cause and the effect, making them look connected. E.g., people who eat vegetables also tend to exercise more. |
| Reverse causation | The causal direction is backwards. It's not that sleeping more causes early death — it's that sick people sleep more. |
| BMI | Body mass index. Weight in kg divided by height in metres squared. |
| RRSP | Registered Retirement Savings Plan. Contributions are tax-deductible; withdrawals are taxed as income. |
| TFSA | Tax-Free Savings Account. Contributions aren't deductible, but all growth and withdrawals are tax-free. |
| RESP | Registered Education Savings Plan. For a child's post-secondary education. Government adds the Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG) — 20% match on the first $2,500/year, up to $500/year. |
| FHSA | First Home Savings Account. Tax-deductible contributions (like RRSP) + tax-free withdrawals for a first home purchase (like TFSA). |
| CPP/QPP | Canada Pension Plan / Quebec Pension Plan. Mandatory payroll deduction; provides retirement, disability, and survivor benefits. |
| EI | Employment Insurance. Provides temporary income after job loss, parental leave, sickness, or caregiving. |
| OAS | Old Age Security. Monthly payment to most Canadians 65+, funded from general tax revenue. |
| GIS | Guaranteed Income Supplement. Additional monthly payment for low-income OAS recipients. |
| CCB | Canada Child Benefit. Tax-free monthly payment to eligible families with children under 18. |
| GST/HST Credit | Quarterly payment to low- and modest-income individuals/families to offset the goods and services tax. |
| CRA | Canada Revenue Agency. Administers tax laws and delivers benefit payments. |
| ESDC | Employment and Social Development Canada. Federal department overseeing EI, CPP, OAS, labour standards, etc. |
| IRCC | Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada. Handles immigration, work permits, study permits, and citizenship. |
| SIN | Social Insurance Number. Nine-digit number needed for work, tax filing, and accessing government programs. |
| POA | Power of Attorney. Legal document authorizing someone to make financial or personal-care decisions on your behalf. |
| OSAP / student loans | Ontario Student Assistance Program (varies by province). Provincial-federal student loan programs. |
| LTB | Landlord and Tenant Board (Ontario). Each province has its own dispute-resolution body for rental issues. |
| WSIB | Workplace Safety and Insurance Board (Ontario). Provincial workers' compensation. Name varies by province. |
| AED | Automated External Defibrillator. Red or yellow boxes in public places; follow the voice prompts. |
| CPR | Cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Chest compressions when someone's heart has stopped. |
| Naloxone | A medication that temporarily reverses opioid overdose. Free kits available at pharmacies across Canada. |
| MAID | Medical Assistance in Dying. Legal in Canada under specific eligibility criteria. |
| PIPEDA | Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act. Federal privacy law for private-sector organizations. |

</details>

## Canada-Specific Notes

- **Healthcare** references assume provincial single-payer health insurance. Drug coverage, dental, vision, and mental health coverage vary dramatically by province — items note where to check your province's rules.
- **Employment law** follows federal standards where noted; most workers fall under provincial jurisdiction. Key differences (e.g., Ontario ESA vs. BC ESA vs. federal Canada Labour Code) are flagged.
- **Tax** references use 2025–2026 figures. Tax brackets, contribution limits, and benefit amounts change annually — check CRA's website for current numbers.
- **Legal** references cite the Criminal Code of Canada (federal) and note where provincial law applies.
- **Benefits** (EI, CCB, OAS, GIS, etc.) have eligibility criteria that change — always verify at canada.ca.
